The test is no longer whether one famous AI lab can disappoint. It is whether the entire investment chain can withstand a change in assumptions. A 30% spending cut is not a forecast; it is a common stress test applied to every origin so the results can be compared. The important distinction is between a loss that a strong balance sheet absorbs and a loss that pushes a fragile borrower across a threshold and stops its purchases altogether.

Most writing on AI risk asks one question: what happens if OpenAI stumbles? It is a fair question. But it is the wrong place to stop. OpenAI is one premise among dozens. Nothing says it is the most likely to fail or the most damaging if it does. A fairer test asks the same question of every firm. If this firm's premise fails and it cuts its AI spending, how much damage does it do to everyone else? Its answer measures each firm's culprit side on one scale.

The test: cut one firm's spending by 30% and follow the money

The rule is the same for every firm. Its AI-related spending falls by 30%. Each supplier loses its share of that spending, as set by the map. A supplier that loses AI revenue cuts its own AI spending by the same share. Its suppliers then feel that cut in the next round. A firm that crosses its break line stops buying altogether. If it was financing-dependent, its lenders lose 60% of what it owes. The rounds repeat until nothing changes. That takes five or six rounds.

Microsoft makes a good worked example. It spends about $108B a year with the suppliers in the map. A 30% cut removes $32.4B in the first round.

  • Nvidia loses about $12.1B, SK hynix $6.6B, Samsung $4.5B, Micron $4.0B and Dell $3.6B.
  • Microsoft is IREN's only contracted AI customer.[42] IREN loses 30% of its contracted AI revenue, which is past its 20% line. It stops buying and its lenders lose about $4.7B.
  • Nebius and Nscale rent much of their capacity to Microsoft.[43, 44] They lose about $1.0B and $2.6B. Neither crosses its line on this shock alone.
  • The suppliers then cut their own purchases. Nvidia's cut reaches TSMC and the memory makers. Their cut reaches ASML, Applied Materials and Lam.

In total, other firms lose $47.1B a year. The first round was $32.4B. So the network added about 45% on top of the direct hit.

With funding ties. Switch on the funding ties from Part 3 and Microsoft's cut costs other firms $53.1B instead of $47.1B. Microsoft's own funding of the labs does not shrink, because a funder pulls back only when it loses revenue. The extra comes from Nvidia and the memory makers. They lose revenue, fund the labs a little less and the labs buy a little less. IREN still breaks. No other firm does.

The biggest shocks start where revenue is thinnest

Run the same test for every firm and a ranking appears. In the chart, the light part of each bar shows the damage if no firm ever broke. The dark part is the extra damage from firms crossing their break lines.

Size does not decide the order. Microsoft, Amazon and Alphabet spend far more than OpenAI. Yet a cut at OpenAI does almost three times the damage. The reason is the gap from Part 1.

A lab's cut is leveraged. OpenAI spends about 2.8 times its revenue with clouds and suppliers. Investors pay the difference. Suppose its revenue falls 30% and it cuts spending by 30%. The first round then removes $42.7B, against only $15B of lost revenue. Anthropic's cut is not leveraged in the same way, because its spending roughly matches its revenue. A 30% cut removes $20.6B.

End users sit at the top of the whole stack. Suppose what users pay the labs falls 30%. The labs lose $35.4B and cut $63B of spending. In turn the clouds cut theirs and Oracle freezes. Each dollar users stop paying removes about $6 of revenue elsewhere. The total is $215B and eight firms break.

With funding ties. Every shock that starts with a lab, a big cloud or end users grows, by 12% to 21%. OpenAI's reaches $155.7B and end users' $243.1B, with nine firms breaking instead of eight: Core Scientific joins. Shocks from the big clouds rise to $40B to $54B. At the top the order does not change, though Anthropic draws level with Amazon at $54.4B, just ahead of Microsoft. Shocks that start with a supplier do not move at all. Nvidia's cut still costs $24.2B and TSMC's $6.1B, because the firms they buy from fund no one. In the chart, the thin orange bar under each firm shows the total with funding ties.

A big cloud's cut is large but mostly absorbed. Microsoft, Amazon, Alphabet and Meta each do $35B to $48B of damage. Their suppliers have high margins and little debt. They take the hit to profit and carry on. The network adds only 18% to 45% to the first-round hit.

A supplier's cut runs upstream. If Nvidia's sales fall 30%, the damage lands first on TSMC ($10.9B), SK hynix ($6.0B), Micron and Samsung. It then reaches the tool makers. The total is $24.2B.

Anthropic can break more firms than OpenAI

In dollars, OpenAI is the larger source of damage. Count the firms that fail instead and Anthropic can come first. The two shocks travel through different parts of the map.

OpenAI's money goes mostly to large, rated firms. Oracle loses 13.8% of its contracted AI revenue. That is three times its 4.3% line, so Oracle freezes new capex. The freeze removes Oracle's own spending, which breaks two firms further down. One is Crusoe, which builds the Abilene site for Oracle. The other is Applied Digital, which leases to CoreWeave and Oracle. Lenders lose about $3.9B, all of it at Applied Digital, because Crusoe's debt is not public.

Anthropic's money runs partly through small, borrowed-money firms. Lambda and Fluidstack depend on Anthropic for most of their contracts.[51, 25] When Fluidstack stops paying, TeraWulf, Hut 8 and Cipher lose most of their tenants. Five firms break. Lenders lose about $11.7B, three times the loss from the OpenAI shock.

With funding ties. Both shocks grow, OpenAI's by $21.0B and Anthropic's by $9.6B. Neither breaks an extra firm. Lender losses stay at $3.9B and $11.7B. So the contrast holds: OpenAI does more damage in dollars, while Anthropic breaks more firms. OpenAI's own cut deepens the most, from 30% to 38%. Two of its largest funders are hit by its cut: SoftBank through its stake and Nvidia through lost sales. Anthropic's own cut stays at 30%, because its revenue now roughly covers its spending and almost none of that spending rests on funders.

One limit matters here. Anthropic's contracts with Google, Amazon and Microsoft are take-or-pay. Anthropic pays whether or not it uses the capacity.[10] So a revenue shortfall would first show up as Anthropic burning its own cash, about $132B at the S-1 date.[10] Only later would it reach the clouds. The model ignores that delay. For Anthropic, it shows where the damage would land, not when.

A second limit is how fragile this result is. It rests on the 20% break line and on press figures for Fluidstack. When all the uncertain inputs are varied together, Anthropic's shock breaks more firms than OpenAI's in only about 6 runs in 10. In the rest it breaks none. The companion page shows the full test. OpenAI's lead in dollars, by contrast, held in every run.

Loops add little; break points add a lot

The circular deals of Part 1 suggest a frightening idea: a shock that feeds on itself forever. According to the map, this cannot happen. Its reason is simple. Payments flow one way: from users to labs, to clouds, to suppliers, to tool makers. No payment in the map flows back up. The circle in Part 1 is closed by equity stakes, guarantees and loans, not by sales. So a spending cut passes down the chain once and stops at the bottom.

Run the test with no firm allowed to break and OpenAI's shock settles at $80.9B. With the funding ties switched on it settles at $98.2B.

That shifts attention from the loops to the finance that closes them. If a lab's value falls, the suppliers that own part of it lose, as Part 5 shows. If a neocloud's contracts weaken, its lenders lose. This test leaves those funding ties switched off. Part 3 switched them on, letting funders that lose revenue hold back money. OpenAI's shock then grows from $134.7B to $155.7B. Even so, the loops settle: they make shocks bigger without making them endless. The order of the biggest shocks barely moves either. End users and OpenAI stay first and second, while Anthropic rises from fifth to third.

What turns a small shock into a large one is a firm crossing its break line. Turn the break lines on and OpenAI's shock grows from $80.9B to $134.7B. Almost all of the increase comes from Oracle's freeze. That freeze rests on one threshold: a leverage line of 5 times EBITDA. Set the line at 7 times and Oracle does not break, so the shock stays near $81B. The jump does not grow smoothly with the size of the cut. It arrives all at once. With the funding ties, break lines take the shock from $98.2B to $155.7B. Either way, a single break adds more than all the loops together.

For an OpenAI shock, the first step comes at a cut of about 10%, when Oracle freezes. An Anthropic shock breaks nothing until the cut reaches about 22%, when Lambda crosses its line. At 25% Fluidstack follows and takes TeraWulf, Hut 8 and Cipher with it. For end users the curve steps up at 10%, 30% and 60%. This lesson is practical. A shock's size matters less than whether it crosses one of a few lines. Any analysis that averages across outcomes will miss these steps.

With funding ties. The steps come sooner. Oracle freezes at an 8% OpenAI cut instead of 10%. It also freezes at a 7% fall in what users pay instead of 10%. An Anthropic shock still breaks nothing below about 22%. At that point, though, three firms go at once: Lambda, TeraWulf and Hut 8. Between the steps the orange curve also climbs faster, because each cut thins the labs' funding too. The lesson stands. What matters most is which line a shock crosses, not its size.

So far the test has measured the damage each firm's failed premise does to others. The same runs also record who receives that damage, how often and in what form. Some firms lose profit. Others run out of money. A third group loses the value of what it owns. A fourth loses because the premise comes true. Part 5 turns the test around and looks at the victims.
