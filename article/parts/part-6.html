A market can be vulnerable before a company actually fails. Margins can narrow, interest costs can rise, power can delay deployment, export rules can strand demand and a more efficient model can change the economics of rented compute. This part asks which pressures are already visible and what happens when several arrive together. It distinguishes a stress scenario from a prediction—and a policy shock from a cash-flow shock.

Damage is only half of risk. Picture two premises. One would wreck the market if it failed, but almost certainly will not fail. The other would do modest harm, but is already cracking. These are different problems. No one can put an honest probability on a premise failing. What can be measured is how much strain each firm already shows. The autumn of 2026 has supplied plenty of evidence. This part reads that strain and combines it with damage. It then runs shocks that hit many firms at once and checks the model against what markets have already priced.

Stress you can see in the numbers

Five signals capture the strain. Each one comes from a firm's own figures and every listed firm is ranked on each.

  • Funding need: negative free cash flow as a share of revenue. A firm that burns cash needs someone else to keep believing.
  • Leverage: net debt over EBITDA, capped at 20 times. Firms with negative earnings and net debt get the cap.
  • Concentration: the largest buyer's share of the firm's AI revenue, from Part 2.
  • Valuation: market value over revenue. A high multiple means the price already assumes the premise comes true.
  • Price trend: the one-year fall in the share price. It records how much belief has already drained away.

The stress score is the average of a firm's five rankings. It runs from 0 for the least strained of the 53 listed firms to 1 for the most strained. It is a ranking, not a probability. Its value is that it applies the same evidence to every firm.

The most strained firms are not the famous ones. IREN, Cipher, TeraWulf, Core Scientific, Applied Digital and Hut 8 all burn cash. They carry debt their earnings cannot cover and depend on one or two tenants. Most have seen their shares fall. SpaceX ranks fourth, mainly on valuation. It is valued at about 87 times revenue while burning $19.6B of free cash a year.[58] WhiteFiber, Nebius, Oracle, CoreWeave and SoftBank come next. At the other end sit Samsung, SK hynix, Sandisk, GE Vernova, Dell and HPE. For them, the premise is currently paying off.

The two leading labs are private, so they cannot be ranked the same way. Still, the signals that do exist are telling. OpenAI's last closed round valued it at about 17 times its revenue.[59] The round it is now raising asks about 25 times.[15] It has committed about 28 times its revenue to compute.[14] Anthropic's May round valued it at about 30 times its first-half revenue pace but about 14 times its third-quarter pace.[10, 12] It owes about $430B of compute it cannot cancel, roughly six years of third-quarter revenue, but holds about $132B of cash.[10] On 30 October OpenAI cut its round from $30B to $20B and its target value from $1.4T to $1.25T.[15] OpenAI would rank near the top on both valuation and funding need. Anthropic would rank high on valuation but low on funding need.

Likelihood against damage

Put the stress score next to the damage each firm causes in Part 4. Two kinds of firm separate: those most likely to fail and those whose failure would matter most.

The chart reveals two different risks. Each calls for a different response.

The likely first break is small. Apart from SpaceX, the most strained firms are neoclouds and landlords. Their own cuts do little damage to the rest of the map, under $2B a year each. When one fails, the hit lands on its lenders and landlords. It also tells the market that contracts with AI customers are less safe than their price assumed. That is more a shock to confidence than a shock to revenue.

The damaging break is less likely but much larger. Here the labs and the big clouds cause $35B to $135B of damage each. A fall in what users pay the labs causes $215B. Their stress is moderate, with two exceptions. Oracle sits high on both axes. SpaceX is strained but connected to few suppliers in the map.

With funding ties. The damage axis stretches for the firms that sit above funded customers. Labs and big clouds rise to $40B to $156B and the end-user shock to $243B. Meanwhile the likely first breaks barely move: a strained neocloud's own cut still costs the rest of the map under $2B. So the two risks stay apart. The ties make the damaging break more damaging. They do not make the likely break more likely.

When many premises fail together

Premises rarely fail one at a time. A weak revenue year at the labs, a cheaper way to train models or tighter credit would each hit many firms at once. The Part 4 test can start from several firms together. Six such joint shocks are worth running. Each can be rerun in the lab. New mixes can be built there too.

The largest totals come from two shocks that start at the top of the chain. An efficiency breakthrough does $186B of damage. Disappointing lab revenue does $180B. That same shock also breaks the most firms: Oracle freezes and seven neoclouds or landlords fail. The largest lender losses, $17.6B, come from tighter credit. That shock does the least revenue damage. But it hits exactly the firms that borrowed. A pullback by the big clouds does $131B of damage yet breaks only IREN. Their suppliers can absorb it.

With funding ties. The order at the top flips. Disappointing lab revenue grows from $180B to $208B and breaks nine firms. Core Scientific joins the list. An efficiency breakthrough grows less, from $186B to $202B. It breaks no extra firm. The difference lies in who is hit first. Weak lab revenue hits the firms most dependent on funders, so the loops amplify it most. An efficiency shock lands first on suppliers, which fund almost no one. The rest grow by 8% to 15%: a cloud pullback to $141B, tighter credit to $69B and a refinancing freeze to $59B. A capex cut by the memory makers does not change at all. Lender losses rise only where an extra firm breaks, to $18.2B for disappointing revenue.

An efficiency breakthrough: cheaper output, but cheaper rent?

Suppose a new model does the same work with half the compute. Its output gets cheaper, so demand for it rises. Whether GPU rents then rise or fall depends on how strongly demand responds. Economists call this the price elasticity of demand. With installed capacity fixed, the rent changes by a factor of k to the power (elasticity minus 1) over elasticity. Here k is the efficiency gain.

If demand responds weakly (elasticity 0.6), doubling efficiency cuts rent from $2.35 to $1.48. That is below break-even and the lenders of Part 5 take the loss. If demand responds strongly (1.5), the same doubling lifts rent to about $2.96. No source settles which case applies. The fall in rents since 2023 points to weak demand. Satya Nadella’s remark that power, not chips, is the constraint points to strong demand.[60]

Credit and rates: the price of the bridge money

Interest rates are already rising. Inflation picked up through the summer. Consumer prices rose 3.4% in the year to August and 3.7% in the year to September. In September alone they rose 0.6%.[61] On 16 September the Federal Reserve raised its rate by a quarter point to 3.75% to 4.00%. It was the first rise in three years.[62] On 28 October the Fed held, but two of its twelve voters, Lisa Cook and Beth Hammack, wanted another rise.[63] In the week before the election, markets priced more than an 80% chance of a December rise. After the 5 November sell-off it fell to about 67%.[64, 65] A strong jobs report on 6 November lifted it back to about 78%.[65]

The table shows what further rises would do to the two most leveraged middlemen. It assumes 30% of Oracle's debt and 70% of CoreWeave's has a floating rate.

A 200 basis-point rise would take CoreWeave's interest cover from 2.21 times to 1.85 times. Rates matter more through Oracle's leverage, which is already 4.48 times earnings.[45] The joint risk also matters. CoreWeave's credit default swap spread reached a record 1,194.5 basis points on 15 October.[66, 67] That price implies about a 63% chance of default over five years, assuming 40% is recovered. Oracle's spread of about 315 implies about 23%.[47] If the two were independent, the chance of both defaulting would be 14.6%. If they move together with a correlation of 0.5, it rises to 20%.

Power delays: chips that cannot be switched on

Satya Nadella has said Microsoft has chips "sitting in inventory that I can't plug in" because buildings lack power.[60] Microsoft cut its 2026 capex guidance to about $170B because third-party data centres were late.[68] A delay costs more than interest. Rents keep falling while the chips sit idle.

Six months idle costs $6,232 per GPU. Of that, $4,759 is lost rent. Across 100,000 GPUs the cost is about $0.62B. The idle-GPU example belongs to Microsoft, not Meta. Satya Nadella said Microsoft had chips “sitting in inventory that I can’t plug in” because power and completed data-centre capacity were not ready. That is not evidence of deliberate hoarding or permanently stranded hardware. It is evidence that capital can be committed and GPUs purchased before the infrastructure needed to earn a return is available.[60] Zuckerberg’s separate remarks concern energy constraints on Meta’s build-out; they do not establish that Meta had warehouses of idle GPUs.[69]

Geopolitics: demand that policy can remove

Under US government pressure, Oracle cancelled a $7B lease of chips to Tencent.[70] At about $1.60 per GPU-hour, that is roughly 500,000 GPU-years. Suppose Oracle re-lets half of it at 80% of the price. It would still lose $4.2B, which is 60% of the contract. Nvidia took a $400M charge on unsold H200 chips in the six months to July, after limited shipments to ByteDance and Tencent.[8] Equipment makers carry the same risk. In the map, 35% of their revenue comes from buyers outside it, including China. That share is an assumption.

Policy: the premise that voters can change

Every premise so far belongs to firms. Will users pay, will capex continue and will collateral hold? One premise sits beneath all of them and no firm controls it. It is the belief that the build-out can continue at its current pace and on today's terms. Behind it sits a second belief: if things go wrong, the state will step in. The 3 November midterms put both in play.[5, 6]

What changed on 3 to 5 November

Congress. Democrats won control of the House and the Senate, where they hold about 54 seats.[71] Most bills need 60 votes to pass the Senate. Anything that passes then faces a presidential veto. Overriding it takes two thirds of both houses.[71]

States. Democrats flipped at least five governorships, including Texas, Ohio, Georgia, Nevada and Iowa.[5] Texas holds about 25% to 30% of the data-centre capacity expected by 2030.[5] Its governor-elect, Gina Hinojosa, has pledged to halt data-centre approvals until the legislature passes new protections.[5] Her Republican predecessor had already paused grid connections and permits for data centres during an audit.[5, 72] Georgia's governor-elect says she will sign an order pausing new construction on her first day.[5]

Voters. Local measures restricting data centres passed in 44 of 45 completed votes.[6] In the AP VoterPoll, about three quarters of voters oppose a new data centre in their own community.[6] That majority holds in red and blue states alike. Campaigns spent more than $380M on television ads that mentioned data centres.[6]

The backlash does not belong to one party. Opposition to local data centres is a majority in both kinds of state. A Republican governor started the Texas pause. A Republican senator, Mike Rounds, co-signed the Democratic proposal on model testing.[71] Democrats are divided too. Senator John Fetterman opposes a national moratorium. He says it would hand the lead in AI to China.[73, 71] The White House is described as the industry's strongest supporter. It has asked AI firms to pledge to pay the full power costs of their data centres. Amazon, Oracle, Google, Meta, Microsoft, OpenAI and xAI have signed.[74]

What has been proposed and how it would reach the map

The table separates what can happen quickly from what cannot. Federal law restricting AI firms needs votes from both parties and a signature. So it is unlikely before 2029. Three things need no new federal law. Governors can pause permits. Local votes can block sites. Congressional committees can call AI chief executives to testify. That is where the near-term risk sits.

The bailout question needs its own note, because it reaches the lenders of Part 5. On 29 October two House Democrats, Greg Casar and Maxine Waters, introduced a resolution, H.Res. 1642.[75, 76] It says insolvent AI firms should go through ordinary bankruptcy. So should the banks that lose money on them.[75] There would be no help from the Fed, the Treasury or the FDIC. The resolution is not binding. But Waters is expected to chair the House Financial Services Committee from January.[77] Binding limits on emergency Fed lending are now a real question.

The position is neither new nor partisan. In November 2025, OpenAI's finance chief floated a federal "backstop" for AI infrastructure.[78] The White House ruled out any bailout and OpenAI dropped the idea.[78] What the resolution changes is how firmly the market can rule out a rescue. Without a backstop, lenders bear the full 60% loss of Part 5. And a run on the weakest firms (Part 7) has nothing to stop it.

Why the labs' own warnings matter here

The labs' premise has two faces. To investors, AI is powerful enough to transform the economy. That justifies the valuations examined in Part 8. To lawmakers, the same leaders say AI is powerful enough to carry serious risks. Anthropic endorsed California's AI safety law, SB 53, in 2025.[79] Its chief executive has defended the case for regulation. Senators Cantwell and Rounds cited reports of frontier models escaping their test environments. They used them to call for federal testing at the national laboratories.[71] Here the culprit-and-victim pattern appears once more. The premise that raises the labs' value also makes them easier to regulate.

What a building slowdown does in the model

A moratorium does not cancel contracts. It stops new construction in some places for a while. In the model, every builder buys less equipment for new sites. Builders here means the clouds, neoclouds and landlords. Equipment means chips, memory, servers, network gear and power equipment. Existing capacity keeps being rented, so the clouds' payments to neoclouds stay the same. Any firm that loses revenue then cuts its own spending, as in Part 4. UBS estimates that pauses put about 30% of the capacity planned for 2028 at risk.[72] Goldman Sachs expects the pauses in Texas, Georgia and Michigan to be temporary.[72] The chart runs one-year slowdowns of 10%, 20% and 30%.

Three results stand out.

On its own, a slowdown hurts earnings, not solvency. A 30% slowdown removes about $190B of revenue a year. That is more than any single firm's cut in Part 4. Yet no firm breaks. The loss lands on the strongest balance sheets: Nvidia about $56B, SK hynix $31B, Samsung $21B, Micron $19B and TSMC $11B.

Combined with closed funding, it threatens solvency. Add the funding squeeze of Part 7 to a 20% slowdown. The damage reaches about $214B and six firms break. Oracle freezes. Applied Digital, Core Scientific, Crusoe, Hut 8 and TeraWulf fail. Lenders lose about $14B. The breaks come from the funding side. Policy adds the size.

The model understates the neoclouds' own exposure. It counts what builders stop buying. It does not count the revenue a builder loses when its own site is delayed. That second channel hits the bitcoin miners that became AI landlords. Bernstein says more than half of the power capacity they hold or plan is in Texas.[72]

The same event also creates winners. Capacity that is already powered, permitted and running becomes scarcer and more valuable. Principal Asset Management made this point after the vote.[5] Owners of working sites gain from the rule that hurts firms still building. Examples include Equinix and Digital Realty. A neocloud with live capacity outside the paused states would gain too. Policy, like every other premise here, turns one firm's loss into another firm's gain.

With funding ties. A 30% slowdown removes about $204B instead of $190B and still breaks no firm. Nvidia's loss does not change. The extra comes from the labs: their suppliers lose revenue, fund them a little less and the labs buy a little less. One test stays on payments only: the slowdown combined with the funding squeeze. That squeeze already cuts OpenAI's and the neoclouds' funding directly, so adding the ties would count the same pullback twice.

What the market has already priced

Everything above describes what could happen. The autumn of 2026 shows the market starting to price it. It is doing so in the order the model suggests, with the most strained and concentrated firms first.

  • 28 April: the Wall Street Journal reported that OpenAI had missed internal sales targets.[80]
  • 9 July: S&P cut Oracle to BBB-, one notch above junk.[36]
  • 16 September: the Fed raised rates for the first time in three years.[62]
  • 8 October: the FT reported OpenAI's annualised revenue at about $50B, not the $68B to $70B reported in late September. That day CoreWeave fell 7.8%, Oracle 5.5%, Broadcom 4.3%, AMD 3.9% and Nvidia 2.9%.[9, 81]
  • 13 October: the IMF named an AI correction as one of its three main financial risks. It counted 14 deals with $300B of equity and $650B of debt, tied to $1.4T of promised spending.[82, 48]
  • 15 October: CoreWeave's five-year credit default swap reached a record 1,194.5 basis points.[67]
  • 22 October: Meta's costs rose 68% and its cash flow turned negative for the first time in over a decade. Meta fell 7.0% and the chip index 6.7%.[83, 84]
  • 28 to 29 October: Alphabet's search revenue grew 12% from a year earlier but was flat on the quarter. Free cash flow was negative. Its finance chief said answers in AI Mode earn less than classic search. The shares fell more than 11%.[85, 86, 87]
  • 30 October: OpenAI cut its funding round from $30B to $20B.[15]
  • Early November: neocloud shares fell 26% to 40% in a month. Over October the chip index fell 15.4% and the Nasdaq 5.3%.[88, 89]
  • 3 to 5 November: Democrats won both houses of Congress and at least five governorships, including Texas. On 5 November the Nasdaq fell 5.5% into a correction. The chip index fell 12.3%, one of its largest falls on record.[1, 4]

Access to new money is closing. Nscale hoped to list at a value of $30B to $35B.[90] Some investors reportedly bid only $16B to $19B.[90] Firmus shelved its listing.[91] From January to October, 30 neoclouds raised about $69B. About 69% of it was debt or similar, according to an unnamed analysis cited by VARINDIA.[92] Gartner expects 80% of neoclouds to lose their independence by 2030, mainly through takeovers.[25] No source reports a default or a broken covenant. The evidence supports "failure has become more likely", not "failure has happened".

Taken together, the map, the shocks and the evidence now point the same way. Most likely to fail first is the premise of small firms that borrowed against one or two customers. The premise whose failure would do the most damage is that users will pay the labs enough, soon enough. A third premise has just become a live risk: that the build-out can continue at its current pace. Yet this autumn's market reactions were larger than any of these numbers can explain. Part 7 shows why. Belief moves faster than money.
