The most dangerous exposure is not always where the largest dollar loss appears. A profitable chip supplier can absorb a missed order; a highly leveraged infrastructure company may not survive the same percentage decline. And when GPUs are financed as collateral, their value depends on how long they remain useful and how much rent they can earn—not simply on what they cost to buy. This part follows the losses into profits, equity, debt and collateral.

Every shock in Part 4 had a source and a set of receivers. But being on the receiving end means different things for different firms. A chip maker loses profit. A neocloud runs out of money. SoftBank loses the value of what it owns. A bank loses what it lent. And a software firm loses when the premise comes true, not when it fails. These are four ways to be a victim, plus a fifth that runs in reverse. Each leaves a different mark.

The first way to lose: running out of money

The chart shows each firm's worst single shock from Part 4. That is the largest share of its contracted AI revenue removed by any one firm's 30% cut. A financing-dependent firm breaks at 20%. Oracle breaks at 4.3%.

Ten firms cross their line in at least one run. Every one of them is a neocloud, a landlord or Oracle. TeraWulf, Hut 8 and Applied Digital each break in three runs. None of the chip, memory, equipment or power companies comes close, even though they lose more in dollars. Nvidia's worst case is $33.2B, from a fall in what users pay the labs. That is only 12.5% of its AI revenue and Nvidia carries almost no debt.

With funding ties. The same ten firms break. Core Scientific breaks in two runs rather than one. Nscale comes closest to joining them: when users cut, its loss grows from 18.5% to 19.9% of its AI revenue, just short of its 20% line. Most worst cases rise by up to five points. Applied Digital's rises most, from 38% to 47%. Oracle's rises from 13.8% to 18.1% and Nvidia's from 12.5% to 13.9%. In the chart the orange part of each bar shows the extra. Still, the pattern does not change: every firm that breaks is still a neocloud, a landlord or Oracle.

The firms that break share one trait. They borrowed to build capacity for one or two customers. Lenders accepted that premise when they lent against the contracts. It is also the premise that fails first.

The second way: profits shrink

Self-funded firms do not break. But their earnings take the hit and their share prices are set on those earnings. The chart turns each firm's worst loss into lost gross profit, shown as a share of its EBITDA.

Oracle stands out again. A 30% OpenAI cut puts a third of its EBITDA at risk. Next come AMD, Marvell and Vertiv at 17% to 20%. Then come Nvidia, Celestica and Digital Realty at 11% to 12%. These firms' valuations depend most on the capex of a few buyers. Several trade at high multiples of past earnings: Nvidia at 23 times, Broadcom 42, Vertiv 47, Marvell 80 and AMD 130.[33] At those levels, a change in the market's view of the next few years moves the share price far more than it moves earnings.

With funding ties. The profit at risk rises for the firms whose buyers lean on funders. Oracle's worst case grows from a third of its EBITDA to 44%, now from a fall in what users pay. AMD and Vertiv rise to 19% to 21%; Nvidia to 14%. Marvell and Celestica barely move. The ranking stays the same.

The third way: what a firm owns loses value

The first move from Part 1 was investing so customers can buy. It leaves suppliers holding equity in their customers. When a lab's valuation falls, those holdings fall too.

For the big platforms, a 30% markdown costs about 1% to 1.5% of their own value. That is painful but survivable. SoftBank is different. A 30% fall in OpenAI's value would cost it about $33B, which is 21% of SoftBank's entire market value.[17, 38] SoftBank sold Nvidia shares to fund that investment.[17] In effect, it swapped a stake in the supplier for a stake in the customer. Gains work the same way in reverse. One secondary source says revaluing Amazon's stakes in OpenAI and Anthropic added $62.8B to its first-half 2026 results.[20] Any gain booked on a customer's rising valuation reverses when that valuation falls.

With funding ties. These markdowns sit outside the payment model, with or without the ties. They are, however, the reason one of the ties exists. Because SoftBank's stake falls with OpenAI, Part 3 assumes SoftBank funds OpenAI less as OpenAI cuts. That single tie adds about $11B to OpenAI's shock.

The fourth way: lending against the premise

At least $1.3T of US data-centre and related debt is outstanding.[18] Utility and infrastructure loans make up about $428B.[18] Syndicated loans account for about $225B and big-cloud corporate credit for another $225B.[18] The fifteen largest lenders hold about $196B between them.[18] Private lenders such as Pimco, Blue Owl and Blackstone sit beside TD, Goldman Sachs, Wells Fargo and JPMorgan.[18]

Against $1.3T, the model's direct lender losses look small. An OpenAI shock costs lenders $3.9B and an Anthropic shock $11.7B. Losses rise to $15.5B when end users cut and $17.6B when credit tightens (Part 6). But these losses are concentrated. They sit in loans against single-customer contracts and against GPUs. GPUs are collateral that ages fast. A loan sized on a five- or six-year accounting life can be underwater long before it is repaid.

With funding ties. Figures for the OpenAI and Anthropic shocks do not change, because no extra borrower breaks. The end-user shock costs lenders $18.2B instead of $15.5B, as Core Scientific joins the failures. Tighter credit still costs $17.6B. Lender losses move only when the ties push one more borrower over its line.

Take a bullet loan, repaid in one sum at the end, of 65 against 100 of chips. If the lender must sell at a 40% discount, it is already 5 short on day one. After 1.93 years the loan is larger than the chips' market value. An amortising loan, repaid in stages, is safer. Its worst gap is 4 per 100 in year one and the gap closes by year three. Whether a GPU pays for itself depends on how long it stays useful.

With a five-year life, the break-even rent is $1.71 an hour. Today's rents cover it. With a three-year life it is $2.50 and today's rents fall short. Lenders are betting on the five-year life. The history of rents points to the shorter one.

How much a lender gets back depends on the age of the chips when the borrower fails. The next chart combines the loan and collateral inputs above. In it, a borrower defaults when its GPUs reach a given age. Its lender then sells them at a 40% discount and recovers what it can.

An amortising lender recovers more than 90% at any age. Its balance falls about as fast as the chips lose value. A bullet lender recovers 74% if the borrower fails after one year, 59% after two and 47% after three. Most neocloud GPU debt was raised in 2025 and 2026.[18, 28, 30] A wave of failures in 2027 or 2028 would therefore meet chips two to three years old. Seen this way, the model's 60% loss on default is harsh for amortising loans. For bullet loans in their fourth year, it is close to the mark.

The firms hurt if the premise comes true

Every premise in Part 1 has a mirror image. If AI keeps memory scarce, memory makers win and device makers lose. If AI agents work, labs win and seat-based software loses. Share prices over the last year show the market betting on both sides at once.

Memory. Standard DRAM contract prices rose 90% to 95% in the first quarter of 2026 (TrendForce).[52] Gartner expects a 130% rise for the full year.[52] HP said memory now makes up 35% of the cost of building a PC, up from 15% to 18% a quarter earlier.[52, 53] PC and phone makers warned of price rises of 15% to 20%.[52] IDC and Gartner expect PC shipments to fall 10% to 11% in 2026. Counterpoint, IDC and Gartner expect smartphone shipments to fall 8% to 13%.[52] Sandisk shares are up 463% and SK hynix 154%. Apple and HP are up 28% and 23%, so the market has not yet punished the device makers.[33] The pain shows up first in the shipment forecasts.

Software. In early February Anthropic launched Claude Cowork, which runs multi-step business tasks with little human input. Within 48 hours, about $285B of software market value disappeared.[54] The software ETF IGV fell more than a third from its September 2025 peak.[54] Over the year ServiceNow is down 42%, Reddit 36% and Adobe 26%.[33, 54] Their premise is that AI adds to seat revenue rather than replacing it. The market doubts that premise more than any other.

Note that the consumer channel works as a brake, not a spiral. Higher memory prices cut device demand. That cuts memory demand and slows the price rise. The real effect lands on chip vendors that sell into both markets.

Intel's data-centre share of product revenue rose from 32.5% to 47.4%.[55] So a 20% cut in AI capex would now remove 9.5% of its product revenue, up from 6.5%.[55] Weaker consumer demand pushes chip vendors deeper into the AI chain. That leaves them more exposed to the premise, not less.

The device channel in the latest results

This channel is no longer a forecast. The September-quarter results show every step. Component prices are up. Device makers' units and margins are down. Chip vendors' consumer lines are shrinking while their AI lines grow.

Two conclusions follow. First, rising component prices do cut device demand and then orders to chip vendors, but only in their consumer businesses. HP's units, Qualcomm's Android handsets, AMD's gaming chips and Intel's PC chips are all down.[53, 56, 57, 55] Second, this does not hold for total revenue, which AI is still lifting. So the loop does not shrink the chip vendors. It changes what they are. Each quarter, more of their revenue depends on the AI premise. Less depends on the consumer market that could cushion them if AI spending fell.

Both sides of every firm on one chart

One last chart brings the two halves together. Its horizontal axis shows how much damage a firm's own 30% cut does to everyone else (Part 4). Up the vertical axis is the largest share of its AI revenue that any other firm's cut removes. Every firm in the payment chain appears somewhere. None sits at zero on both.

Three groups appear. On the right sit the labs and end users, the large culprits. The labs are also 30% victims of their own users. In the middle are the big clouds and Nvidia. They are large culprits and moderate victims and none of them breaks except Oracle. Oracle sits in the danger zone on both axes. At the top left are the neoclouds and landlords. They are small culprits in dollars but large victims and they are the only firms that break. Each group's premise is the next group's risk.

With funding ties. The labs, the big clouds and end users move right by 12% to 21%. Oracle and the neoclouds move up a few points. No firm changes group.

Knowing who would cause damage and who would receive it is half of risk. The other half is likelihood. Which premises are closest to failing? And what happens when several fail at once, as they tend to? Part 6 reads the answer from the strain already visible in each firm's numbers.
