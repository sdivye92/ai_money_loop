Interdependence is not the original source of the investment risk. It is the mechanism that can transmit and amplify it. A supplier's investment in a customer, a lender's loan against a contract and a cloud provider's promise of capacity can make firms look safer because they are connected. But the cash-flow arrows and the financing arrows are not the same thing. This part separates them and measures how much the financing links really amplify a shock.

Part 2 drew the market as a map of who pays whom. A map like that has a shape as well as numbers. Network science has standard ways to read it. Count the loops. Find the hubs. Then ask whether the firms at the centre are the ones most at risk. Each question has a clear answer here. Two of the answers run against intuition.

Payments run one way. Finance closes the loops

Much of the worry about this market concerns circular deals. A supplier invests in a customer, then the customer spends the money with the supplier. The IMF has warned that about $1.4T of such deals could deepen a market correction.[48] In network terms a circle like this is a loop: a path that starts at one firm, passes through others and comes back to where it began.

The payments alone contain no loops at all. Every payment in the map runs from a firm closer to end users toward a firm further from them. Users pay the labs. Labs pay the clouds. Clouds pay the neoclouds and the chip makers. Chip makers pay TSMC and the memory makers, who pay the tool makers. The longest chain has six steps, from end users through OpenAI, Microsoft, CoreWeave, Nvidia and TSMC to ASML. In technical terms the payment network is a directed acyclic graph. A dollar that leaves a firm as a payment never returns to it as a payment.

Finance changes that. The deal database records 40 ties that are not payments for goods or services: equity stakes, loans and guarantees or backstops. Most run against the flow of payments, from suppliers and lenders back toward the firms that buy from them. Add them to the 120 largest payments, those of at least $1B a year. At once, 43 loops of two to four firms appear. Allow longer loops and the count rises to 295. The figure below shows the 15 firms the short loops pass through. Start with payments only, then add the finance.

The loops are not spread evenly. Nvidia sits in 27 of the 43 and OpenAI in 23. Two finance ties close 18 loops between them: Nvidia's money into OpenAI and Nvidia's stake in Anthropic. In 34 of the loops a single finance tie is enough to close the circle. The pattern repeats each time. A supplier puts money into a customer. The customer spends it with a cloud, which buys the supplier's chips.

Loops matter for two reasons. The first is that they make part of today's revenue. Money that starts as a supplier's investment can return to that supplier as sales. So a supplier's revenue says less about outside demand than it appears to.

The second reason is that loops decide whether a loss can come back to the firm that sent it. In a network without loops, a cut travels down the chain and stops at the end. That is why the tests in Part 4 settle within five or six rounds. With loops, the same cut can return. If OpenAI cuts its spending, Nvidia sells fewer chips. Nvidia also owns a stake in OpenAI and has guaranteed part of its Ohio build.[8, 23] So the same decision hits Nvidia a second time, through what it owns and what it has promised. Part 4's payment test leaves that second hit out.

Part 2 showed how funding ties work on six firms. On the real map, each funding tie gets a weight: the share of the recipient's spending that rests on that funder. A funder that loses revenue then provides less, in proportion to its loss. The companion page Inside the Model lists every weight and lets any shock run with the ties on or off. With these loops switched on, a 30% cut at OpenAI costs other firms $156B a year instead of $135B. OpenAI ends up cutting 38% of its spending rather than 30%. Across shocks that start with a lab, a cloud or a neocloud, the loops add 10% to 33%.

The loops do not run away. Funders would have to pull back nearly four times harder than their own revenue loss for the system to feed on itself. One new lever does appear. Nvidia supplies about half the outside money OpenAI has from the funders in the database, counting its stake and its guarantee.[8, 23] If Nvidia withheld 30% of that, other firms would lose about $96B a year. Oracle, Crusoe and Applied Digital would break, as they do when OpenAI cuts its own spending. That result depends on how hard funders pull back: at half the strength, nothing breaks. Part 5 measures what stakes and guarantees are worth when a premise fails. How quickly markets price it is the subject of Part 7.

The hubs: where the money passes

A hub is a firm that much of the money passes through on its way somewhere else. Network analysts measure this with betweenness. Take every pair of firms where money can travel from one to the other. This map has 406 such pairs. Find the most direct route between each pair, counting a large payment as a shorter step than a small one. A firm's betweenness is the share of those routes that pass through it.

Only 11 of the 48 firms sit on any route between two others. The rest only start routes or end them. End users start them and the tool makers end them. Five firms carry most of the traffic. Nvidia lies on 19% of the routes, TSMC on 13%, Amazon on 12%, Microsoft on 9% and OpenAI on 8%. Each is a junction between layers. Nvidia links the builders to TSMC and the memory makers. TSMC links the chip designers to the tool makers. Amazon and Microsoft link the labs to the chip makers. Microsoft also links them to the neoclouds.

It seems natural to expect the hubs to do the most damage when they cut. They do not. At least, not because they are hubs. Across the 31 firms tested in Part 4, betweenness and damage caused move together only loosely. Their rank correlation is 0.54, where 1 would mean the two measures put the firms in exactly the same order and 0 would mean no relation. A much simpler measure does far better. The amount a firm spends with other firms in the map predicts its damage almost perfectly, with a rank correlation of 0.99. TSMC is the second-busiest junction, yet a 30% cut in its spending costs other firms only $6.1B a year. OpenAI is only the fifth, yet its cut costs them $134.7B.

The reason lies in what a junction does. A firm on many routes passes money along. When it cuts, it removes its own spending and nothing more. The map does not multiply a cut by the cutter's position. It passes on what it is given. At every step some of that leaks to buyers outside the map. The size of the first cut decides the damage, along with whichever break lines it crosses. Position does not.

The centre is not where firms break

The second intuition comes from banking. Since 2011 the Basel Committee has scored the world's largest banks partly on how interconnected they are, because the failure of a well-connected bank spreads further.[50] It is tempting to read this map the same way and treat Nvidia, Microsoft or Amazon as the weak points. The tests in Part 4 point the other way.

Take, for each firm, the largest share of its AI revenue it loses in any of the 31 tests. Then ask which network measures line up with that loss. The firms that receive the most money, have the most ties or rank highest on PageRank lose the smallest shares. PageRank, the method Google first used to rank web pages, follows money along every route and asks where it piles up. Its rank correlation with the worst-case loss is −0.46. Betweenness shows no link at all, at −0.04.

Only one measure rises with the loss: buyer concentration, at +0.49. It adds up the squared shares of a firm's buyers, with buyers outside the map counted as one more. A value of 1 means a single buyer takes everything. Five equal buyers give 0.2.

The firms at each end of the ranking make the point plainly. Ten firms break in at least one test: Applied Digital, TeraWulf, Hut 8, Oracle, Crusoe, Lambda, Fluidstack, Cipher Mining, IREN and Core Scientific. None of them ranks among the ten most central firms by PageRank. Those ten are tool makers, chip and memory makers, a server builder and a maker of power equipment. None of them loses more than 13% of its AI revenue in any test.

At the other end, seven of the eight firms most dependent on one buyer do break. TeraWulf and Hut 8 lose up to 95% of their AI revenue when Anthropic cuts, because their capacity is leased to Fluidstack and Fluidstack serves Anthropic.[24, 25]

Why does the centre hold? Centrality measures traffic. A firm at the centre sells to many buyers, each taking a slice. If it loses one, the others remain. Breaking takes two things at once. One buyer must be large enough for its cut to matter. The firm's cushion must also be thin enough for that loss to cross a break line, which borrowed money, a loan covenant or a single lease can set. Both conditions sit at the edge of the map, in the neoclouds and landlords that hang from one or two lines.

Two firms show the rule from opposite sides. Alphabet is highly concentrated in the map, because Anthropic is the largest buyer of its AI capacity. It still holds, because its cushion is large and its break line is far away. Oracle is the reverse case. It is the best connected of the firms that break. But about 46% of its backlog comes from OpenAI and its debt has been cut to the lowest investment grade.[7, 36] Concentration and a thin cushion break it, central or not.

What this means for anyone with money in the chain. Centrality answers a useful question: where a shock will pass on its way through the chain. It does not tell you who breaks. For that, watch two numbers at each seller. One is the share of its revenue that comes from its largest buyer. The other is how much revenue it can lose before it hits a covenant or a funding wall. Then watch the finance ties that close loops, because they are where a loss can come back to the firm that sent it.

The limits are plain. These are rank correlations across a few dozen firms in one map. They describe how risk is arranged in this market today; they are not a law of networks. Still, the patterns survive the uncertainty in the inputs. Across 500 runs that redraw every estimated link and threshold, none of the ten most central firms ever breaks. In every run buyer concentration rises with the worst-case loss while PageRank falls with it. Betweenness never shows a clear link. Only the count of dependent firms that break moves: in most runs it is between four and seven of the eight. The companion page Inside the Model lists every measure for every firm and explains how each was computed.

How far each firm's failure travels

The table below reads the map the way a shock reads it. Each link is weighted by how much of the seller's AI revenue depends on that buyer, not by dollars. Two of its columns measure how much damage starts at a firm. The rest describe its position. Read together, they repeat the part's two findings. The firms whose failure costs others most are the big spenders, while position adds little.

Table: systemic importance of all 48 firms, ordered by damage if the firm stops buying
  • Damage if it stops buying ($B a year). The revenue all other firms lose if this firm stops all AI spending and each firm it hits cuts its own spending in proportion to what it lost. Break lines are switched off. It is the linear version of the test in Part 4 at full size, so a 30% cut costs about 0.3 of it. This is the dollar answer to the question of who is the bigger culprit.
  • With financing feedback ($B). The same full stop, once funders are allowed to pull back in proportion to the revenue they lose. The gap to the previous column is what the loops add. It is largest for Amazon, Anthropic, Alphabet, Oracle, Meta and Microsoft, at 10% to 14%, because their suppliers also fund the labs. Suppliers lower in the chain gain nothing: their own suppliers fund no one.
  • Out multiplier. The same chain reaction, counted in shares instead of dollars: the sum, across all other firms, of the share of AI revenue each one loses. Technically it is the firm's column of the inverse (I − W)−1, less the firm itself. It counts how many firms' worth of revenue a failure wipes out, whatever their size. That is why Anthropic ranks above OpenAI on it: its failure would hit small neoclouds that depend on it almost entirely.
  • Out weight. The first round only: the shares of each supplier's AI revenue that this firm pays directly, added up.
  • In weight. The share of the firm's own AI revenue paid by firms in the map. Buyers outside the map pay the rest. A value near 1 means the firm lives entirely inside the chain.
  • PageRank. Follows shocks along these weights and asks where they end up. It differs from the PageRank above, which follows dollars.
  • Betweenness. The number of firm pairs whose strongest shock path passes through the firm. A path's strength is the product of its weights.
  • Inbound HHI. How concentrated the firm's buyers inside the map are. Unlike buyer concentration in the first table, it ignores buyers outside the map. So a firm with one buyer in the map scores 1 even if outsiders pay most of its revenue.

The payment map has no loops, so W is nilpotent: its spectral radius is 0 and each multiplier is the sum of a few rounds. With financing feedback the radius rises to 0.54, still below the value of 1 at which loops would feed on themselves.
