The financial network moves through contracts and payments. The market's belief network moves through prices, narratives and the fear of being last to sell. That second network can reprice an entire sector before any supplier loses a dollar of revenue. The volatility of autumn 2026 is not just background noise: it is evidence that expectations have become a transmission channel of their own.

On 22 October Meta raised the low end of its capex range.[84] In the payment map, that is good news for chip makers. It means more orders. Yet that day Intel, AMD, Sandisk and Marvell each fell 8% or more.[83] The chip index fell 6.7%.[83] Nothing in the flow of money explains it. Something else does. So far this series has followed money. But money is the slower of two forces in this market. The faster one is belief.

Investors and lenders know the premises of Part 1 are risky. So they watch for the first sign that one is failing. When a sign appears, they ask what it means for every firm that shares the premise. They do not wait to see what it changes in anyone's accounts. This part measures how far reactions have run ahead of the numbers and explains why. It then feeds that behaviour back into the model. Falling prices close funding. Closed funding cuts spending. And cut spending makes the original fear come true.

Two networks: one carries money, the other carries belief

The map in Part 2 links firms that pay each other. A second, invisible map links firms that share a premise. Broadcom and CoreWeave do not pay each other. But both are priced on the belief that AI spending keeps rising. ServiceNow and Anthropic are not each other's suppliers. Yet one belief, that AI agents work, lifts one and threatens the other. News moves along the payment map one step at a time. Along the belief map, it reaches everyone at once.

That difference sets the size of a shock. In the payment map, a 30% cut at a neocloud costs the rest of the chain a few billion dollars a year (Part 4). In the belief map, the same event is evidence about the premise behind about $14T of supplier value and $12T of platform value.[38] What matters is how strongly prices respond to that evidence. The last two years answer that question.

Reactions that ran ahead of the news

Three patterns run through these nine events.

The trigger is usually information, not a loss. DeepSeek, Claude Cowork, Musk's post and the election changed no firm's revenue that day. They changed what investors believed about the future. In January 2025 Nvidia lost about $589B of value in a single day.[93] The cause was a claim about training costs.[93]

Good results do not protect a firm whose premise is in doubt. HP beat estimates and raised its outlook. It still fell 9.3%, because its unit sales fell 16% on memory costs.[53] Meta beat revenue estimates and fell 7.0%. For the first time in over a decade, its spending had outrun its cash.[83, 84]

The reaction spreads to firms the news does not touch. Meta's higher capex range on 22 October should have helped its suppliers. The payment map predicts a small gain for them. Prices moved the other way. Investors read the news as a sign that AI spending would have to slow.

On 8 October prices followed the premise, not the exposure

The FT report of 8 October is the cleanest test, because the news was a single number. OpenAI's annualised revenue was about $50B, not the $68B to $70B reported in late September.[9, 11, 81] Against $68B that is a gap of 26.5%.[9] The model can show what the gap would do if OpenAI cut its spending by 26.5% for good. About $126B a year would leave the chain and Oracle would freeze. Each firm's lost revenue can be turned into lost gross profit, as a share of its EBITDA. That gives the hit each firm would take. The chart compares it with how far each share price actually fell that day.

Two groups appear. The firms that are truly exposed fell by a fifth to a third of their profit at stake. Oracle has about 30% of its EBITDA tied to the full cut and fell 5.5%.[81] CoreWeave has 32% and fell 7.8%. Nvidia has about 10% and fell 2.9%.[81] The market seemed to price a one-in-four chance that the shortfall was permanent. That is a calm reaction. The firms with little exposure fell by about their whole stake or more. Broadcom has about 2% of EBITDA at stake and fell 4.3%.[81] Amazon has under 2% at stake. It was down 2.3% during the day[94] and closed more than 1% lower.[95] Their prices did not reflect their own risk. They reflected membership of a group whose premise had just been questioned.

With funding ties. The same 26.5% cut removes about $147B instead of $126B and breaks the same three firms. Profit at stake rises: Oracle's to 38% of EBITDA, CoreWeave's to 41% and Nvidia's to about 10%. Measured against those larger stakes, the exposed firms priced in a seventh to just over a quarter of their risk, not a fifth to a third. That makes their reaction calmer still. The lightly exposed firms barely change. Broadcom's stake rises only to about 3%, so its fall still far exceeds it. The ties sharpen the contrast this test was built to show.

This is the belief network at work. It explains why a small miss at one firm moves the whole market. Investors do not sell what is exposed to the miss. They sell what is exposed to the idea.

5 November: a policy shock read through the belief network

The election gave a second clean test. On the day after the vote the Dow rose 3%, its best post-midterm day since 1982.[1] Data-centre suppliers were already slipping on permit worries. On Thursday 5 November the selling spread. The Nasdaq fell 5.5% into a correction.[1, 2] Meanwhile the S&P 500 fell 4.4% and the chip index 12.3%.[1, 4] In one day the Nasdaq-100 lost about $2.3T of value.[1, 96] Reports linked the fall to fears that the Democratic wins would slow data-centre construction. Other forces were at work too. Investors took year-end profits on the year's winners ("they're shooting the generals", in Nomura's phrase). Retail investors sold at record levels. Two chip designers reported weak results.[97, 1, 2] The policy share of the fall cannot be separated from the rest.

To isolate what policy alone could justify, the chart compares each firm's fall that day with one measure. That measure is the profit the firm would lose if data-centre building slowed by 30% for a year. This is the upper end of the UBS estimate from Part 6.

Two things differ from 8 October. First, exposed firms priced in a much larger share of their stake. Nvidia fell 9.3% against about 21% of profit at stake, close to half.[98, 2] AMD, Marvell, Broadcom and Intel fell by between half and five sixths of theirs. On 8 October, similar firms priced in only a fifth to a third. Three explanations fit. The market may have seen a policy slowdown as longer-lasting than a revenue miss. It may have priced a slowdown larger than 30%. Or other selling that day may have deepened the fall. Second, the belief channel was visible again. Arm fell 13.1%, with almost no exposure to US data-centre building in the map.[4, 99] CoreWeave fell 9.8%, even though[4] the slowdown test leaves its rented capacity untouched. Its real exposure is one the model does not count: its own new sites.

With funding ties. These stakes barely move. A building slowdown hits suppliers first and suppliers fund almost no one, so the loops have little to work on. Nvidia's stake stays near 21% of profit and AMD's and Marvell's do not change. The comparison with 5 November is the same with or without the ties.

Policy news should hit harder than revenue news. A revenue miss can be made up next quarter. A rule changes conditions for every firm at once and for years. A promise of no bailout removes the floor under the weakest firms. Investors already knew the build-out was risky. Now they had a reason that applied to all of it.

Why reactions run beyond the numbers

None of this means investors are foolish. Each mechanism below is well documented. All are rational for the individual, even when the combined result is an overreaction.

Everyone knows it is risky. When every investor knows the neoclouds are fragile, each one's best move after a bad sign is to leave first. The firm survives only if enough people stay. That is the logic of a bank run (Diamond and Dybvig, 1983).[100] It applies to any firm that must keep raising money. The first failure does not need to be large. It only needs to be seen. Bank runs are stopped by a lender of last resort. For AI firms, the White House (in 2025) and the incoming House majority (in 2026) have both said there will be none.[78, 75, 76] Nothing stands in the way of a run.

One sign updates the whole premise. OpenAI's revenue gap is information about OpenAI. It is also the best evidence available on whether AI revenue is arriving at all. Investors cannot check every firm, so they follow the signal and each other. Economists call this an information cascade (Bikhchandani, Hirshleifer and Welch, 1992).[101] It explains why Broadcom fell more than its exposure.

Prices are bets on fragile growth. A firm priced at 50 times earnings is priced on many years of growth. The simplest valuation formula sets value equal to earnings divided by (required return minus growth).[102] At a 9% required return and 7% growth, that gives 50 times earnings. If expected growth slips to 6%, the multiple drops to 33 times, a fall of a third. At 5% it is 25 times, a fall of half. A one-point change in long-run expectations moves the price far more than any one quarter's numbers.

Losses hurt more than gains please. People weigh a loss about twice as heavily as a gain of the same size (Kahneman and Tversky, 1979).[103] After a long run-up, a stock that has doubled carries a large paper gain. Its owner fears losing it. The first bad sign triggers selling to protect it. Those 52-week gains in Part 5, from 77% at TSMC to 463% at Sandisk, are exactly this kind of gain.[33]

Some selling is forced. A fund allowed to hold only investment-grade bonds must sell if Oracle is cut to junk. Its view of Oracle does not matter. Leveraged investors must sell to meet margin calls when prices fall. That pushes prices down further (Brunnermeier and Pedersen, 2009).[104] None of this selling is a judgement about the company.

Stories move faster than facts. "AI overinvestment" is a narrative investors increasingly recognise. Once a narrative takes hold, each new fact can be read as proof of it (Shiller, Narrative Economics, 2019).[105] A post from Musk with no new data was enough to send CoreWeave and Nebius bonds to record lows.[106]

There is a further tension in the industry's public posture. In June, Anthropic called for a coordinated, verifiable pause in frontier-AI development; in September, Anthropic chief executive Dario Amodei and OpenAI chief executive Sam Altman both argued for slowing the pace of progress. Yet a third-party release log counted six OpenAI and four Anthropic model releases in September alone. That count includes different model tiers and product categories, not ten frontier breakthroughs, so it should not be mistaken for a measure of capability growth. But it captures the incentive problem: a lab can warn that the race is moving too quickly and still face powerful commercial reasons not to be the one that stops shipping while competitors continue.[113, 114, 115]

Prices change the facts. Falling prices are not only a reaction to fundamentals. They change them. Firms that depend on new money can raise less of it. George Soros called this reflexivity. It is the step that turns fear into lost revenue. And the model can measure it.

How one small failure reaches everyone

Part 4 found that a small neocloud's failure does little direct damage through payments. If TeraWulf failed on its own, its suppliers would lose about $0.1B a year. Its lenders would lose about $3.1B. Run logic points to a different channel. When lenders see one borrowed-money AI firm fail, they cut new money to all of them. The test below adds that step. After the first failure, every financing-dependent firm loses part of its new funding. It then cuts the spending that outside money paid for by the same share.

The pattern is the point. A failure that costs $0.1B in payments grows into $11B to $22B of lost revenue once lenders react. One to four more firms fail. Lenders lose $8B to $18B. A CoreWeave failure grows from $26B to $38B. The second wave hits the same firms every time. Hut 8 and TeraWulf go first, then Applied Digital, Core Scientific and Cipher. They have the least cash and the most borrowed spending. This is how "small firms failing" reaches the rest of the market. It travels through lenders and belief, not through the small firm's own purchases.

Why this test and the next have no funding-ties version. Lenders cutting new money after a failure is a funding tie at work. This test sets that pullback directly. So does the funding squeeze below. Running the ties from Part 3 on top would count the same pullback twice. So both stay as they are. Read them as the funding-tie story told with market-sized pullbacks instead of the model's proportional ones.

The runway explains the order. At its current pace, WhiteFiber has about two months of cash. Hut 8 has about three, CoreWeave about five and Applied Digital about eight.[33] A firm in this position does not need to lose a customer to fail. It only needs its next round of funding to arrive late or small. For the most stretched firms, missing payments for a couple of months is not a distant scenario. A couple of months is their entire cushion.

When prices feed back into spending

The last step closes the loop. Those October and November moves were not just losses on paper. Lower prices mean smaller funding rounds and costlier debt. OpenAI cut its round from $30B to $20B.[15] Nscale hoped to list at $30B to $35B but met bids of $16B to $19B.[90] Firmus pulled its listing.[91] So the test below asks what happens if these funding conditions last for a year.

  • Every financing-dependent neocloud and landlord raises 35% less outside money. That is the midpoint of their 26% to 40% share-price fall.[34, 107] Each cuts the spending that outside money paid for by the same share.
  • OpenAI raises a third less, matching its round cut. Investors fund about 65% of its spending, so it cuts its spending by about 22%.

No customer cancelled anything in this test. Funding got harder because prices fell. Yet the result is about as large as a 30% cut by OpenAI itself (Part 4). That is reflexivity in numbers. Fear about the premise produces the very spending cut it was afraid of.

In the lab, both funding cuts can be changed to see what follows.

The value at stake is far larger than the revenue at stake

The listed firms in the map are worth about $34.6T. Seven of them are worth about $22.6T: Apple, Nvidia, Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon, Meta and Broadcom. That is roughly a third of the S&P 500's $68.5T.[38] The neoclouds and landlords are the firms most likely to break first. They are worth about $0.3T, under 1% of the map.[38, 108] So the smallest group is the trigger for the largest one.

If the autumn's falls repeated across the whole map, about $3.7T of market value would disappear. The revenue at stake in the model's worst joint shock is about $0.2T a year, with or without funding ties. That gap is the growth bet from the formula above. Prices contain many years of expected growth. So a change in belief about the premise moves value far more than any single year's revenue. And that value is not just on paper. It backs the share sales, convertible bonds and stock-funded deals that pay for the build-out. When value falls, the bridge money from Part 1 shrinks.

The payment model shows where damage lands. What the belief network shows is why it arrives faster and spreads wider than payments alone would carry it. And 5 November showed that a change in policy travels along it as fast as a change in revenue. One question remains and every investor in this market is asking it. Can revenue catch up before belief runs out? Part 8 takes it on.
