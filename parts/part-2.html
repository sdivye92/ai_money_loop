The underlying risk is hard to see when every company is examined on its own. One firm calls its spending investment; another calls the resulting contract revenue; a lender calls that contract collateral. Put the firms together and the same dollar appears several times—as a commitment, a backlog, a loan and an expected future purchase. This part maps those dependencies so we can distinguish genuine end demand from money and promises circulating inside the build-out.

News stories describe this market one deal at a time: Nvidia and OpenAI, Google and Anthropic, Oracle and its backlog. That view hides what decides who gets hurt. A firm's risk depends less on any one deal than on two questions. Whose spending does its revenue come from? And how much of that revenue can it afford to lose? Answering both for every firm needs a view of the whole market at once.

This part builds a simple model of the whole market. Everything that follows in the series relies on it. The model has three pieces. First comes a map of who pays whom: for every firm, the share of its AI revenue that comes from each buyer. Next comes a break line for every firm: how much revenue it can lose before it stops buying from its own suppliers. The third piece is the funding ties: who has invested in, lent to or guaranteed whom. A funder in trouble can hold back money. Its customer then has less to spend. Nothing else is assumed. The last section of this part shows exactly how the pieces work together. It runs one shock twice, first on payments alone and then with the funding ties, so the effect of the loops is visible. Part 3 measures that effect on the real market. The tests in Parts 4 to 7 leave the funding ties switched off, so their figures are payments-only results.

The map covers every listed firm with a material stake in the build-out. It also includes the private firms that matter most. On purpose, it reaches well beyond the companies in the news.

Money gets bigger as it moves down the chain

The map rests on one question asked of every seller: what share of its AI revenue comes from each buyer? When a firm discloses the answer, the map uses it. Microsoft and Meta are 26% and 16% of Arista's revenue.[39] CoreWeave is 69% of Applied Digital's contracted leases.[40] OpenAI is 46% of Oracle's backlog.[7, 36] When nothing is disclosed, the seller's AI revenue is split across its likely buyers in proportion to their capex. Before that split, 30% is set aside for buyers outside the map, such as enterprises, governments and Chinese firms. Adding up the flows layer by layer gives the picture below.

Three features stand out. Each comes straight from the numbers, not from a view about any company.

The labs pay out more than they take in. Users pay the two leading labs about $118B a year.[9, 10] Spread their contracts evenly over time and the labs owe clouds, neoclouds and chip makers about $211B a year. OpenAI owes about $142B and Anthropic about $69B. That is 2.8 times OpenAI's revenue. Investors cover the difference. Anthropic is the exception. Its third-quarter revenue was $16.96B, a pace of about $68B a year, which roughly matches what its contracts cost.[12] Operating cash flow is already positive.[41] The model uses that third-quarter pace for Anthropic and OpenAI's end-September pace, so both labs are measured at the same point in time.

The clouds spend far more on suppliers than the labs pay them. In the last twelve months the mapped buyers spent $781B on capex.[33] Chips take about $241B of the big clouds' payments. Memory takes about $187B. That is before counting what the neoclouds spend.

Suppliers pay their own suppliers. Chip designers pay TSMC and the memory makers about $109B a year. TSMC and the memory makers then pay the tool makers. So a cut at the top travels three or four steps before it stops.

How the links were built and how much of the map is disclosed

The map has 306 buyer-to-seller links. 20 are disclosed shares, worth about $309B a year. 19 are estimates from contracts or reports, worth about $83B. 267 are allocations in proportion to capex, worth about $844B. Payments by buyers outside the map and by end users account for the remaining $496B. Disclosed links cover the most important joints: labs to clouds, the neoclouds' customers and the landlords' tenants. Allocations cover the suppliers, which do not name their customers. For chip designers, each one's payments to TSMC and the memory makers are weighted by its cost of goods on AI products, so a designer cannot pay its suppliers more than it spends making its chips. The appendix gives the rule for every seller.

The firms themselves: who is tied to whom

The layer picture adds firms together, while the network below keeps them apart. Each circle is a firm, grouped by layer around the ring. Lines are the ties between firms. Even without numbers, the picture shows three things.

Nvidia, Microsoft and Amazon sit at the centre. Nvidia has more ties than any other firm, with 23. Nearly every builder pays it and it has invested in several of its customers. Microsoft and Amazon have 19 ties each.

The neoclouds hang from a few lines. Most have one or two paying customers and a loan. This is the concentration problem of the next section, drawn as a picture.

Money flows one way and finance closes the circle. Follow the payment lines. They only run from labs toward suppliers. The lines that run back, from Nvidia, Microsoft, Amazon and Google into the labs and neoclouds, are equity and backstops. Part 3 counts the loops this creates and what they mean for risk.

Dependence rises toward the middle of the chain

How much can one decision elsewhere hurt a firm? Two numbers give a good first answer. One is the share of a firm's AI revenue that comes from its largest buyer. The other is the share of its spending paid with outside money: equity, loans and guarantees rather than its own cash flow. That shows how much it depends on funders. It is also the number the funding ties in the model use, later in this part. Below, both appear for each group of firms.

What emerges is a ladder. At the safe end are the big chip, memory and equipment makers. They sell to many buyers and fund themselves. One rung along are the big clouds. They depend on one or two labs for 20% to 46% of their backlog, but they generate their own cash. Oracle and Amazon are the exceptions. Their capex exceeds their operating cash flow by $28.7B and $46.9B, so outside money pays for 38% and 22% of it.[33, 20] At the fragile end are the neoclouds and landlords. For most, one or two customers make up 60% to 100% of contracted revenue.[28, 42, 43, 40, 44] Outside money pays for half or more of their spending. These are the firms a decision made elsewhere can break.

The labs sit outside this ladder. They sell to millions of users, so no single buyer dominates. Yet outside money pays for 65% of OpenAI's spending. For Anthropic the share is about 1%. No firm in the map leans harder on its funders than OpenAI. That matters once the funding ties enter the model, later in this part.

Two kinds of firm: those that absorb a loss and those that pass it on

A firm that loses revenue faces one of two outcomes. If it funds itself, it takes the hit to profit and keeps going. If it relies on lenders, lost revenue can cut off its funding. Then it stops buying from everyone. These are the break lines, the second piece of the model. They treat the two cases differently, using each firm's own numbers. A third case, a firm squeezed by its funders without losing any revenue, comes with the funding ties later in this part.

Self-funded firms have positive earnings and debt below five times earnings. They break only if lost revenue pushes debt above five times EBITDA, which is earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation. At that level ratings fall and borrowing gets expensive. For most suppliers, it takes a loss of more than half their AI revenue. Oracle is the exception. Its debt is already 4.48 times earnings.[45, 36] S&P cut it to BBB- in July.[36, 46] The trigger for a further downgrade is estimated at 4.5 times.[36, 47] Losing just 4.3% of its contracted AI revenue would push it past 5 times. At that point the model assumes Oracle freezes new AI capex.

Financing-dependent firms have negative earnings, debt above five times earnings or a business built on borrowing against contracts. That includes every neocloud and landlord in the map except the two REITs. These firms break when they lose 20% of their contracted AI revenue. They then stop buying and their lenders recover only 40% of what they lent.

The 20% line comes from CoreWeave, the one neocloud with accounts detailed enough to test. Its quarterly EBITDA covers its interest about 2.2 times.[28, 27] Assume a covenant, a loan condition, set at 1.5 times, which is common. Losing about 19% of revenue would then breach it. The chart runs this calculation for different customer shares and cuts.

How the model works, step by step

With the pieces in place, the model can be stated in full. It is a set of simple bookkeeping rules. Nothing in it is hidden or tuned to give a chosen answer.

The inputs. For each firm the model holds three things. One is its AI revenue at stake: the yearly revenue it earns from the build-out. Another is the share of that revenue paid by each buyer, taken from the map. Last comes the firm's break line, from the previous section.

The rules. A shock starts when one firm cuts its AI spending by some share, say 30%. Then the same rules repeat, round after round. The first four follow payments only. Rule five adds the funding ties and is explained after the first worked example.

  1. Suppliers lose their share. Each supplier of the cutting firm loses that share of what the firm paid it. A supplier paid $60B a year by a firm that cuts 30% loses $18B.
  2. Suppliers pass the loss on. A supplier that loses 9% of its AI revenue cuts its own AI spending by 9%. Its suppliers feel that cut in the next round.
  3. Break lines turn a cut into a stop. A firm that crosses its break line stops buying altogether. Its suppliers lose everything it paid them.
  4. Lenders take the loss. If a broken firm depends on borrowing, its lenders lose 60% of what it owes.

The rounds continue until no firm's cut changes. In the real map that takes five or six rounds. The model then reports three numbers: the revenue every other firm loses in a year, the firms that break and what lenders lose.

How losses propagate

loss of firm i in a round = sum over its buyers j of (j's cut) × (what j pays i in a year)

firm i's cut in the next round = (its loss) ÷ (its AI revenue at stake)

if firm i crosses its break line: its cut becomes 100% and, if it borrows, lenders lose 60% of its debt

A worked example

Take a small chain of six firms, with round numbers chosen to show the mechanics. A lab spends $100B a year: $60B with a big cloud and $40B with a neocloud. The cloud earns $200B of AI revenue. It spends $100B with a chip maker and $50B with a memory maker. The neocloud earns $50B, with 80% from the lab. It spends $30B with the chip maker and carries $20B of debt. The chip maker earns $400B and spends $80B with a foundry.

Untick the break line and run the shock again. The neocloud then cuts its spending by 24% instead of stopping. So the chip maker loses $7.2B from it rather than $30B. Total damage falls to about $54B. So the network alone adds about 80% to the first hit. One break line then adds another $27B on top. This is the pattern Part 4 finds in the real map: with payments alone, break lines do most of the damage.

Adding the funding ties: where the loops come from

In the run above, money moves one way. A supplier in trouble never makes its customer spend less. Nothing can come back to the lab. The real market is not built like that. Nvidia, Microsoft, Amazon, Google and SoftBank fund the labs that buy from them. Banks and private credit fund the neoclouds. Those funding ties run back up the chain. They add a fifth rule.

  1. Funders in trouble hold back money. A funder holds back the same share of what it provides as the share of revenue it has lost. An investor whose stake falls with its customer holds back the share by which that customer cuts. The customer then has less to spend, on top of any cut it makes because of lost revenue. A funding cut makes a firm buy less but does not break it on its own. Break lines still test lost revenue only.
Funding feedback

funding cut of firm r = sum over its funders f of (f's share of r's spending) × (f's stress)

r's spending = (1 − its cut from lost revenue) × (1 − its funding cut)

Take the same six firms and the same 30% cut. Now give the lab two funders. It spends $100B a year but earns $40B, so $60B comes from outside. The chip maker supplies $30B of that through a stake and a guarantee, as Nvidia does for OpenAI. An investor supplies the other $30B, as SoftBank does. Run the shock again and compare it with the payments-only run above.

Side by side, the two runs show what the loops do. With payments only, the shock settled in three rounds at $81.3B. Adding the funding ties stretches it to 13 rounds and $97.6B, 20% more. The extra comes from the lab cutting deeper, 40.8% instead of 30%. Most of it runs through the investor, whose pullback tracks the lab's own cut. The chip maker's loop is weaker. It loses about a tenth of its revenue, so it holds back only about a tenth of its funding.

The loop settles because each pass returns only part of the one before: here about a fifth. A loop that returned the whole amount would never settle. The shock would grow without limit. Whether the real map is near that edge is a question about its shape. Part 3 answers it. The answer is no. Without the break line, the funding ties add 31% rather than 20%. When no firm breaks, the loops are a larger part of the story.

What the model leaves out. It is deliberately simple. Its limits matter. It follows payments and funding, not prices, so share falls and lender nerves are not in it; Part 7 adds them. It spreads each contract evenly over its life, so it shows where damage lands, not when. It assumes a firm cuts spending in the same proportion as it loses revenue. It sets aside 30% of each supplier's AI revenue for buyers outside the map. Its results are what-if tests, not forecasts.

Run the model yourself. The companion page Inside the Model runs the same code on the same data. Pick any firm, any size of cut or any assumption and see who loses and who breaks. A switch adds the funding ties, so every shock can be run both ways. The page also sets out how the inputs were chosen, how sensitive the results are and what the model cannot tell you.

Where to check it. The appendix sets out the rules as equations. It also lists every seller with the share paid by each buyer. Every firm's financial inputs appear there with sources. So do tests of how much the results move when the assumptions change.

By now the map shows who pays whom, while the break lines show how much each firm can lose. Together they allow the test this series has been building toward: cut one firm's spending, follow the money and see who breaks. Before running it, the map itself deserves a closer look. Its shape, apart from its numbers, already says where money circles and where risk sits. Part 3 reads that shape.
