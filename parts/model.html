The model behind The AI Money Loop · Data to 5 November 2026

Inside the Model

A simple model of who pays whom across 59 firms in the AI build-out. This page shows the whole of it: the rules, the math, the data and its limits. It also lets you run it yourself.

The model asks one question: when one firm's bet on AI goes wrong, what happens to everyone else? It answers by tracking who pays whom across 59 firms, from the AI labs to the makers of chip-making machines. No reader should have to take such a model on trust. So this page does three things. It lets you run the model with your own settings. It sets out the model in full, step by step and then in equations. And it shows where every input comes from and what the model cannot tell you.

How the model works, in plain words

The model is bookkeeping. It tracks who pays whom. It then tracks what happens when one payer pays less. Nothing in it is fitted to produce a chosen answer.

Step 1: the map. For each of the 59 firms, the model holds its AI revenue at stake. That is the yearly revenue the firm earns from the build-out. It also holds the share of that revenue paid by each buyer. Together these give the yearly payment from every buyer to every seller.

Step 2: a shock. One or more firms cut their AI spending by some share. Users can also pay the labs less.

Step 3: the rounds. Each supplier of a cutting firm loses its share of the cut. A supplier that loses 9% of its AI revenue cuts its own AI spending by 9%. Its suppliers feel that in the next round. The rounds repeat until nothing changes.

Step 4: break lines. Some firms do not cut gently. A firm that crosses its break line stops buying altogether. If it depends on borrowing, its lenders lose most of what it owes.

Step 5: the result. The model reports the revenue all other firms lose in a year, the firms that break and what lenders lose.

The model in equations

For readers who want the formal version, here it is in full. The notation is standard; each symbol is explained once.

Model variables\[ \begin{aligned} A_i &= \text{AI revenue at stake of firm }i\;(\text{USD billions/year})\\ s_{ji} &= \text{share of }A_i\text{ paid by buyer }j\\ F_{ji} &= s_{ji}A_i=\text{yearly payment from buyer }j\text{ to seller }i\\ c_j &= \text{share by which buyer }j\text{ cuts its AI payments} \end{aligned} \]

A shock is a starting vector c0. In each round t, every firm's loss and its next cut are:

Loss propagation\[ \begin{aligned} L_i(t) &= \sum_j c_j(t)F_{ji}\\ c_i(t+1) &= \max\left(c_i^0,\min\left(1,\frac{\epsilon L_i(t)}{B_i}\right)\right) \end{aligned} \]

Here Bi is the revenue base against which a firm sizes its own cut. For most firms it is Ai. TSMC, the memory makers and Intel, whose spending is firm-wide capex, use total revenue instead. By default the spending response \(\epsilon=1\): a firm that loses 9% of its AI revenue cuts its AI spending by 9%. Rounds stop when no cut changes by more than \(10^{-9}\). In the real map that takes five or six rounds.

Break lines. Two kinds of firm break in different ways.

Break thresholds\[ \begin{aligned} \text{Self-funded:}\quad &\frac{\mathrm{lev}_0\cdot\mathrm{EBITDA}} {\mathrm{EBITDA}-gL_i}>5 \quad(7.5\text{ for the two REITs})\\ \text{Financing-dependent:}\quad &L_i\geq 0.20A_i\\ \text{On breaking:}\quad &c_i=1,\quad \text{lender loss}=0.60\times\text{debt} \end{aligned} \]

Solve the first rule for L and divide by A to get each self-funded firm's break line as a share of its AI revenue. For Oracle, reported leverage is 4.48 times, gross margin 64% and EBITDA $34.4B. Its line comes out at 4.3% of its contracted AI revenue. For Nvidia it is 97%.

Why 20% for the financing-dependent firms. CoreWeave is the one neocloud with accounts detailed enough to test. In its latest quarter, revenue was $2.575B, interest $0.640B, net income −$0.626B and depreciation about $1.4B. That gives EBITDA of about $1.41B, which covers interest 2.21 times. Assume a covenant at 1.5 times, which is common. Suppose too that 20% of cash costs fall with revenue. Then a loss of x of revenue lowers EBITDA by x(R − 0.2 O), where O is cash operating cost. The covenant breaks at \(x=\frac{\mathrm{EBITDA}-1.5\times\mathrm{interest}}{R-0.2O}=19\%\). Rounded, that gives the 20% default. The lab lets you move it.

What "damage" means. The headline figure is \(\sum_i L_i\) at the end of the rounds: the revenue all other firms lose in a year. Apply only c0 and the same sum gives the first-round hit. Their ratio is the network's amplification.

Why the loops add so little. With break lines switched off, the update is linear. Its end point is the Leontief solution \(\mathbf{c}=(\mathbf{I}-\epsilon\mathbf{W})^{-1}\mathbf{c}^{0}\), where \(W_{ij}=\frac{F_{ji}}{B_i}\). In this map no payment flows back up the chain. So W is nilpotent: its spectral radius is 0 and the inverse is a finite sum. A shock passes down each layer at most once. The circular part of the deals runs through equity, guarantees and loans. The section on financing feedback below adds them and shows how much they change. Only the break lines are non-linear. They do most of the damage.

Three extensions

Scenario shock assumptions\[ \begin{aligned} \text{Builder slowdown:}\quad &\text{equipment purchases are cut by }d\\ \text{Lender pullback:}\quad &c_o^0=1,\qquad c_k^0=\phi x_k\\ x_k&=\min\left(1,\frac{\text{capex}-\text{operating cash flow}}{\text{capex}}\right)\\ \text{Autumn 2026 squeeze:}\quad &c_k^0=0.35x_k\quad(\text{neoclouds and landlords})\\ &c_{\mathrm{OpenAI}}^0=\frac13\times0.648 \end{aligned} \]

Where the data comes from

AI revenue at stake. For big clouds and neoclouds it is contracted backlog spread evenly over the contract life: five years unless the firm discloses one (6.1 years for Amazon). Suppliers use the AI share of trailing revenue times trailing revenue. Labs use revenue: about $50B for OpenAI (run rate at the end of September) and $67.8B for Anthropic (third-quarter revenue of $16.96B times four).

Who pays whom. The map has 306 buyer-to-seller links. Each link carries a code, shown in the tables below:

  • S 20 links are disclosed shares, worth about $309B a year. Examples: OpenAI is 46% of Oracle's backlog, CoreWeave 69% of Applied Digital's leases.
  • E 19 links are estimates from contracts or press reports, worth about $83B.
  • G 267 links are allocations. A supplier that does not name its customers has its AI revenue split across likely buyers in proportion to their capex. First, 30% is set aside for buyers outside the map, such as enterprises, governments and Chinese firms. For chip designers, payments to TSMC and the memory makers are weighted by cost of goods on AI products.

Disclosed and estimated links cover the joints that matter most: labs to clouds, the neoclouds' customers and the landlords' tenants. Allocations cover the suppliers. That is the main weakness of the map. The tables below show which links are which.

Financials. For each listed firm: trailing twelve-month revenue, operating cash flow, capex, free cash flow, debt, cash, gross margin and leverage, from stockanalysis.com statistics pages dated 26 October to 5 November 2026. Firms that reported a new quarter after those dates use the release. Market values come from companiesmarketcap.com in US dollars. Every source is listed in the appendix of The AI Money Loop.

How far the model can be trusted

Three things bear on whether the results deserve trust. One is how the inputs were chosen. Another is how the results compare with what actually happened. The last is how much they move when the inputs change. Here is each, with its limits.

Nothing is fitted to an outcome. No input was tuned to match a result. Revenue, debt, margins and buyer shares come from filings and reports. The 20% break line comes from CoreWeave's accounts and an assumed covenant. So the model cannot be made to look right by adjusting it. Its weak points are the inputs themselves, above all the 267 allocated links.

A rough test against markets. Two market events in autumn 2026 offer a partial test. On 8 October, OpenAI's revenue came in about 26.5% below reports. Among firms the model marks as exposed, the share-price falls lined up with the model's exposure. CoreWeave had about 32% of its EBITDA at stake and fell 7.8%. Oracle had about 30% and fell 5.5%, while Nvidia had about 10% and fell 2.9%. Firms with little exposure, such as Broadcom and Amazon, fell more than the model predicts. So the model ranked direct exposure well and missed the sentiment channel, which it does not try to capture. Over the following month, the group the model marks as most fragile, the neoclouds and landlords, fell 26% to 40%. The chip index fell 15.4%. That fits the model but does not prove it: markets can be wrong. Other causes were also at work.

Sensitivity. The next section tests how much the results depend on the inputs that are least certain.

What real validation would need. Real validation means outcomes: a covenant breach, a default or a capex freeze at the firms and thresholds the model points to. None has happened yet. Quarterly filings will offer a partial test. When a big buyer trims capex, as Microsoft has, the model predicts which suppliers should feel it and by how much. Until such evidence exists, treat the results as structured what-if tests, not forecasts.

How sensitive are the results?

A model built on estimates is only as useful as its weakest input. So two tests were run. The first changes one assumption at a time across a wide range. A second test changes all the uncertain inputs at once, many times over. It then looks at the spread of results. Both use the same code as the lab.

Three assumptions matter most.

  • Oracle's leverage line. At 5 times EBITDA, a small loss tips Oracle into a capex freeze. Move the line to 7 times and Oracle no longer breaks. The OpenAI shock then falls from $135B to $81B. This single threshold explains much of OpenAI's lead as a source of damage.

  • The break line for borrowed-money firms. At 20% or below, Anthropic's shock breaks five firms. At 25% or above, it breaks one or none. The end-user shock goes from 12 breaks at a 10% line to 2 at 40%.

What holds and what does not

Findings that hold in every run:

  • A 30% cut at OpenAI does more damage than a 30% cut at any big cloud (1,000 of 1,000 runs).
  • Users paying the labs less is the largest single-origin shock (1,000 of 1,000).
  • Only neoclouds, landlords and Oracle ever break. That held across all 31 single-firm shocks in 300 separate runs.
  • An OpenAI shock breaks Oracle, Crusoe and Applied Digital in every run.

Findings that do not hold reliably:

  • "Anthropic breaks more firms than OpenAI" holds in about 6 runs in 10. In the rest, Anthropic's shock breaks no firm at all. The result depends on the 20% break line and on press-reported figures for Fluidstack.
  • "Anthropic's shock costs lenders more than OpenAI's" also holds in about 6 runs in 10, for the same reasons.

What these tests cannot do. They test the inputs, not the structure of the model. Payments still flow one way. Firms still cut in proportion to lost revenue. Prices and sentiment are still left out. Disclosed links are held fixed, so an error in a disclosure would not show up here.

The shape of the network

The article's Part 3 reads the shape of the payment map with standard tools from network science. This section sets out how each measure was computed and gives the numbers for every firm.

The payment graph. Each link in the map worth at least $10M a year becomes an arrow from buyer to seller, weighted by its yearly value. That gives 232 arrows among 48 firms. Other firms in the panel are investors, lenders and software or device makers that sit outside the payment chain. The graph has no directed cycle: in technical terms it is acyclic. Its longest chain runs six steps, from end users through OpenAI, Microsoft, CoreWeave, Nvidia and TSMC to ASML. One consequence is mathematical. Written as a matrix of buyer-to-seller shares, the map is nilpotent, so any cut dies out after at most as many rounds as the chain is long.

Loops. Loops are counted on the graph drawn in the article: the 120 payments of at least $1B a year plus 40 finance ties from the deal database. These are 17 equity stakes, 20 loans and 3 guarantees or backstops. Simple directed cycles were listed with Johnson's algorithm. There are 43 of two to four firms: 12 of two, 14 of three and 17 of four. Allowing any length gives 295. Of the 43 short loops, 34 contain exactly one finance tie, 8 contain two and 1 contains three. The payments on their own contain none.

Betweenness. For every ordered pair of firms where money can travel from the first to the second (406 pairs), take the shortest route, where a link's length is one over its yearly value in $B. A firm's betweenness counts the routes that pass through it. Only 11 firms lie on any route.

PageRank. Computed on the weighted payment graph with the usual damping factor of 0.85. It ranks firms by where money accumulates when it follows every route, so firms at the bottom of the chain rank highest.

Buyer concentration. The Herfindahl index of a firm's AI revenue across its buyers: the sum of each buyer's squared share. Revenue not paid by firms in the map counts as one more buyer. A value of 1 means one buyer pays everything.

Outcomes. Damage caused is the yearly revenue other firms lose when a firm cuts its AI spending by 30%. Worst-case loss is the largest share of a firm's AI revenue lost in any of those 31 tests. Breaks counts the tests in which the firm crosses its break line. All three use the baseline settings.

Financing feedback: when trouble travels back up the chain

The model above lets stress travel one way only, from a buyer to its sellers. A seller in trouble never makes its customers spend less. In this market that is not quite true. Nvidia, Amazon, Microsoft, Google and SoftBank fund the labs that buy from them. Banks and private credit fund the neoclouds. A funder that loses revenue may provide less. Its customers then have less to spend. That is the reverse effect. It is what turns the deals into loops. Switch on With financing feedback in step 2 of the lab to add it.

How it works. Each funding link from the deal database gets a weight φ. It is the share of the recipient's spending that rests on that funder: the share of its spending paid with outside money (g) times the funder's share of that outside money, by committed amount. OpenAI pays 64.8% of its spending with outside money. Nvidia's $30B stake and its guarantee of up to $105B make up 52% of the outside money in the database. So Nvidia's weight on OpenAI is 0.648 × 0.52 = 0.34. A funder's stress is the share of its own AI revenue it has lost. It is 1 if the funder has broken. A recipient's spending then falls by κ times the weighted stress of its funders. Anthropic's share is about 1%: its third-quarter revenue of $16.96B, about $68B a year, roughly covers its spending in the map. So every weight on Anthropic is close to zero.

Financing feedback\[ \begin{aligned} p_f&= \begin{cases} 1,&f\text{ has broken}\\ \max\left(p_f^0,\min\left(1,\frac{\epsilon L_f}{B_f}\right)\right),&\text{otherwise} \end{cases}\\ p_{\mathrm{SoftBank}}&=\min(1,m\,c_{\mathrm{OpenAI}})\\ p_{\mathrm{lenders}}&=\min\left(1,\lambda\frac{\text{lender losses}}{103}\right)\\ x_r&=\min\left(1,\kappa\sum_f\phi_{fr}p_f\right)\\ c_r&=1-\left(1-\max\left(c_r^0,\min\left(1,\frac{\epsilon L_r}{B_r}\right)\right)\right)(1-x_r) \end{aligned} \]

The last line treats lost revenue and lost funding as two separate limits on what a firm can spend. With \(\kappa=0\) the model is exactly the payments-only model. A funding cut makes a firm buy less. It does not break the firm by itself: break lines still test lost revenue only. Lender stress from losses on neoclouds and landlords reaches only those borrowers. A direct lender pullback, set in the lab, reaches every borrower.

Two judgements in the weights. AMD's warrants for up to 10% of AMD are left out: they lower what OpenAI pays for chips but are not money it can spend now. The reported $350B of Nvidia financing for OpenAI is replaced by what was signed, a guarantee capped at $105B. Only funders in the database count toward the shares. Funders outside it would shrink every weight, which works like a lower κ.

What the feedback changes

At the default settings (\(\kappa=1\), SoftBank link on, λ = 1), the loops add 10% to 33% to the damage of every shock that starts with a lab, a cloud or a neocloud. A 30% cut at OpenAI costs $156B instead of $135B. OpenAI ends up cutting 38% rather than 30%. A 30% fall in what users pay costs $243B instead of $215B and breaks one more firm, Core Scientific. Shocks that start low in the chain, at TSMC or the memory makers, change not at all: their losers fund nobody.

The loops do not run away. Linearise the system with break lines switched off and its matrix gains a spectral radius of 0.54 (0.41 without the SoftBank link). The payments-only matrix has 0. Below 1, every shock settles at a finite size. The radius reaches 1 only if funders pulled back 4.0 times harder than their revenue loss (11.2 times without SoftBank). Loops make shocks bigger. They do not make the system unstable at any plausible setting.

A new kind of shock: a funder pulls back. With the feedback on, a funder can start a shock without cutting any purchase. Here is how much of its funding each funder must withhold before the first firm breaks.

  • Nvidia: first break at a 23% pullback (Applied Digital, Crusoe, Oracle).
  • SoftBank: first break at a 53% pullback (Applied Digital, Crusoe, Oracle).
  • Amazon: first break at a 59% pullback (Applied Digital, Crusoe, Oracle).
  • Apollo: no firm breaks even at 100%, because Anthropic, its main borrower in the map, now leans on almost no outside money.
  • Banks and private credit: first break at a 34% pullback (Core Scientific).
  • Alphabet: first break at a 27% pullback (Hut 8, TeraWulf).
  • Microsoft: no firm breaks even at 100%.

Nvidia's position as OpenAI's largest funder makes it the strongest lever. Withholding 30% of its funding costs other firms about $96B, nearly three quarters of what OpenAI's own 30% cut costs. It breaks the same three firms, because it pushes OpenAI past the 10% cut that freezes Oracle. That result rests on the weights, so it is the first to check in the table below.

What the feedback still leaves out. Valuations: a fall in OpenAI's value cuts Nvidia's and SoftBank's wealth before it cuts any funding. Timing: committed equity is often paid in tranches, so a pullback may take quarters. And funding cuts that break a firm by draining its cash, which the break lines here do not test.

What the model cannot tell you

  • Prices and sentiment. It follows payments only. Share prices, lender nerves and investor fear are outside it.
  • Timing. It spreads contracts evenly over their lives, so it shows where damage lands, not when. Take-or-pay contracts would delay some losses, notably Anthropic's.
  • Behaviour. It assumes firms cut spending in proportion to lost revenue. Real firms may cut more, less or later. Move the spending-response slider to see the range.
  • Allocated links. 267 of 306 links are allocations, not disclosures. Where suppliers do not name customers, the split is an estimate.
  • Break lines. Covenant terms are private for most neoclouds. The 20% line is calibrated on CoreWeave alone.
  • Forecasts. Its results are what-if tests. They say what would happen if a firm cut by a given amount. How likely that is lies outside the model.
