The evidence points to a dangerous imbalance, not an inevitable collapse. Valuations and infrastructure commitments assume years of growth; the weakest firms depend on a small number of customers and continued access to credit; and the market has shown how quickly belief can change. The final question is whether revenue and productive use can catch up with the capital already committed—and what happens if they do not.

Seven parts have built one picture. Most likely to fail first is the premise of small firms that borrowed against one or two customers. The premise whose failure would do the most damage is that users will pay the labs enough, soon enough. And markets react faster and harder than the money justifies, whether the trigger is a failing premise or a change in policy. Three questions remain. Can revenue catch up with the prices already paid? Which popular claims about this market survive the evidence? And what should anyone with money in this chain do differently?

Can revenue catch up with the prices already paid?

A share price or a funding round is a bet on future revenue. Divide market value by current revenue and the result shows how much of the future a price already assumes. A firm valued at 5 times revenue needs only modest growth. A firm valued at 60 or 87 times needs years of very fast growth without a stumble.

The highest multiples sit at both ends of the chain. SpaceX, which includes xAI, is valued at about 87 times revenue.[58] Palantir is at about 61 times and Arm at about 46.[33, 38] Nebius and TeraWulf are above 30 times. The labs' last closed rounds put them at 17 and 30 times. OpenAI's new round asks about 25 times, while Anthropic's third-quarter pace brings it to about 14.[59, 15, 12] Nvidia, one of the market's most highly valued firms, sits at about 14 times.[33] Its multiple is no higher than either lab's.[33]

For OpenAI the arithmetic is concrete. Assume a 25% net margin and a value of 25 times earnings. The $1.25T target value then needs $200B of revenue. From $50B, that means four times as much: 32% growth a year for five years.

No published, sourced forecast says which path is realistic. The labs release no usage or token data, so a slowdown in usage cannot be tested directly. Revenue is the only hard evidence on demand.[9] For OpenAI it is about $50B, against the $68B to $70B reported in late September and a year-end target of $70B or more.[81, 9]

Where it is heading: four paths

Putting probabilities on these paths would be false precision. But each has early signals that can be watched.

Revenue catches up. Lab revenue grows fast enough that no break line is tested. The neoclouds refinance and the loops stay quiet. Signals: OpenAI closes its round above $20B at $1.25T. Anthropic lists at or above its last private value. Oracle's leverage stays under 4.5 times.[46]

Slow grind. Revenue misses continue and funding costs rise. The weakest firms fail one at a time through dilution, distressed sales and takeovers. Gartner's forecast of 80% consolidation describes this path.[25] Signals: more listings cut or pulled, covenant waivers in filings, CoreWeave's credit spread above 1,000 basis points for months and neocloud contracts moved to bigger firms.

Policy squeeze. State pauses and local votes slow building in Texas, Georgia and other states for a year or more. Power costs shift onto the builders. The new Congress opens hearings into AI firms' commitments and debt. On its own, this cuts supplier earnings (Part 6). Combined with closed funding, it breaks the weakest firms. Signals: the length of the Texas pause after 19 January and how many states follow. Watch too for the first subpoena of an AI chief executive and any binding limit on emergency Fed lending.

Break event. One line is crossed. Oracle is cut to junk or a neocloud misses a payment. The model shows how much the damage grows once a line breaks. Forced selling by investment-grade holders follows and lenders pull back. Signals: a downgrade, a missed coupon or a customer cancelling a large contract.

The cushions are real. Anthropic holds about $132B of cash.[10] The big clouds have profitable core businesses and can absorb large losses. Nvidia's Ohio guarantee only starts as each phase is completed. Many headline amounts are not binding. Together they describe a structure that is fragile at specific joints, not one that is doomed.

What the evidence teaches: how to count exposure

The most practical lesson of this series is a better way of counting. Each rule below corrects a mistake that headline numbers invite.

  1. Count by firmness, not by headline. A signed take-or-pay contract and a letter of intent are different things. Nvidia's $100B letter became a $30B investment.[8, 35] About $1.9T of the deal amounts in the database is not binding.
  2. Look through to the payer. A loan to a neocloud is a bet on its customer. A lease to TeraWulf is a bet on Fluidstack, then on Anthropic and Google's backstop. Lenders already price it this way. The risk sits with the customer's premise.
  3. Measure concentration at the seller. Ask what share of a seller's revenue comes from its largest buyer. Above 40% is a warning. Concentration sets how small a cut can break the seller (Part 2). Do not use centrality as a stand-in: the most connected firms are among the least likely to break (Part 3).
  4. Map who funds whom. A funder in trouble can start a shock without cutting a single purchase. If Nvidia held back 30% of what it has committed to OpenAI, the model puts the damage at about $96B a year, with the same three firms breaking as when OpenAI cuts (Part 3). Funding ties also make every large shock 12% to 21% bigger.
  5. Watch break lines, not averages. Covenants and rating triggers turn a slide into a jump. The same 30% cut by OpenAI does $81B of damage if no firm breaks. It does $135B if Oracle freezes (Part 4). With funding ties the figures are $98B and $156B. The loops add about $17B to $21B, the break about $54B to $58B.
  6. Price collateral on economic life. A GPU loan sized on a six-year accounting life is fragile if rents halve every two years. Break-even rent is $1.71 at five years and $2.50 at three (Part 5).
  7. Separate stress from default. Dilution, distressed sales and takeovers are more likely than missed payments. They change who loses and when.
  8. Count both sides of every firm. A firm that profits from the premise also helps create it. A firm outside the payment chain can be hurt when the premise comes true. Memory makers' gains are device makers' costs. Lab success threatens software (Part 5).
  9. Treat policy as a shared premise. A permit pause or a no-bailout rule changes the terms for every firm at once. It moves prices through the belief network, even where direct exposure is small. On 5 November Arm fell 13%, with almost no exposure to US building (Part 7).[4, 99]
  10. Do not read the risk as one company's. OpenAI causes the most damage in dollars. Anthropic can break more firms. Small neoclouds and landlords are the most strained. The same sellers are exposed through different buyers (Parts 4 and 6).

Circularity multiplies risk. It is not proof of fraud. Many of these deals will work. The point is to count them correctly.

Conclusion: a market built on each other's beliefs

Go back to 5 November. Nothing broke that day. No AI company defaulted and no large contract was cancelled. Still, the Nasdaq fell 5.5%, the chip index fell 12.3% and about $2.3T left the Nasdaq-100 in a single session.[1, 96] The sell-off does not prove that the AI market has already failed. It shows how much value can be repriced when investors begin to doubt the assumptions supporting it.

The primary risk is the scale and speed of the wager. Hundreds of billions in annual capital spending, trillions in backlogs and commitments, rising borrowing and valuations that require years of exceptional growth have accumulated in a short period. The five largest spenders plan $803B to $828B of capex for 2026; the mapped big clouds carry about $2.47T of contracted backlog; US data-centre and related debt is put at more than $1.3T in the article's evidence base. These numbers are not interchangeable, and they are not all AI-only obligations. Together, however, they show how much future demand the market is asking to arrive. The question is whether utilisation, customer revenue and margins can catch up before hardware ages, financing costs rise or more efficient architectures change the economics.

Interconnectedness makes the investment risk more dangerous; it is not the reason the risk exists. The payment network runs one way, and the model's financing loops add about 12% to 21% to major shocks before settling rather than feeding on themselves (Part 3). The sharper discontinuity comes when a firm crosses a break line. A small, order-dependent neocloud can stop buying, strand a supplier's expected revenue and cause lenders to reassess other borrowers with similar funding structures. The weakest firms may be small in market value but sit at joints where contracts, collateral and credit meet.

The trigger need not be a lab default. It could be a customer slowdown, a tighter credit market, a power delay that leaves expensive GPUs idle, a policy change that removes a market, or a new training method that reduces the amount customers are willing to pay for compute. Oracle's cancelled Tencent lease shows that geopolitics can change a commercial premise; Nadella’s idle-chip remark shows that buying GPUs is not the same as putting them to work. In each case, the immediate loss may be local. The repricing can be much wider because investors are judging the same growth story across the whole sector.

That is why sentiment matters. Markets can move before defaults, covenant breaches or contract cancellations. The belief network carries a common shock faster than payments do, and falling valuations can then make funding harder, which forces spending cuts and validates part of the original fear. The industry's own tension—public warnings about the pace and risks of AI alongside a continuing race to release models—adds to the sense that commercial incentives and collective caution are pulling in different directions. It is not proof of a crash; it is another reason not to treat optimistic forecasts as guaranteed outcomes.

None of this makes a crash inevitable. AI can create real value, revenue may catch up, and the strongest firms have balance sheets capable of absorbing losses that would sink weaker borrowers. But the market is no longer a bet on one company or one model. It is a bet that many valuations, contracts, power projects, financing arrangements and customer plans will all hold together. The danger is that the investment bubble has expanded faster than the evidence of realised returns. It may burst abruptly if confidence breaks, but it could also deflate over time through lower valuations, cancelled projects, restructurings and returns that fall short of what investors have priced in. Neither outcome is certain; the longer expectations outrun operating economics, however, the less room the system has for a painless adjustment.
