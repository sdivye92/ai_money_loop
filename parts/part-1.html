The AI investment cycle is the starting point, not an afterthought. The scale of investment, valuations, contracts and borrowing has grown faster than the evidence that the returns will justify it. The question is not whether AI has real uses. It does. The question is whether the financial commitments built around those uses can be supported by revenue, utilisation and margins before the next change in technology, demand or policy. The network matters because it can turn that mismatch into contagion.

On Thursday 5 November 2026 the Nasdaq fell 5.5% and entered a correction.[1, 2, 3] The index of chip stocks fell 12.3%, one of its worst days on record.[1, 4] About $2.3T of value left the Nasdaq-100 in a single session.[1] Yet nothing had broken. No AI company had defaulted. No large contract had been cancelled. What changed was a belief. Two days earlier, voters had given both houses of Congress and at least five governorships, including Texas, to a party that had campaigned on slowing down data-centre construction.[5, 6]

Markets only fall that hard on a belief when a lot rests on it. This series is about what rests on it. Since January 2023, the companies building AI have bought from each other, invested in each other, lent to each other and guaranteed each other's debts. The scale is new for the technology industry. Chip makers have funded the labs that buy their chips. Cloud providers have taken stakes in the customers that rent their capacity. Small firms that few investors knew two years ago have borrowed billions against contracts with one or two customers.

One question runs through all nine parts: when one firm's view of the future proves wrong, who else pays? Three tools were built to answer it. First comes a database of 92 deal legs signed between January 2023 and November 2026, each marked by how binding it is. Second, a financial panel covers 59 firms from every layer of the build-out, from the AI labs to the makers of chip-making machines. Third, a simple model of the market, built step by step in Part 2. With it, any firm's spending can be cut and the effect traced through everyone else. All three are explained in the appendix. A companion page, Inside the Model, sets out the model in full and lets readers run it on the same data. But the story itself starts with something simpler: a single dollar.

Every firm's numbers rest on someone else's belief

Follow one dollar. A business pays OpenAI for a subscription. OpenAI has promised Oracle about $300B for computing capacity over several years.[7] Oracle borrows to build data centres and buys Nvidia systems to fill them. Nvidia pays TSMC to make the chips and SK hynix for the memory on them. SK hynix and TSMC buy machines from ASML to make more. And Nvidia has put $30B of equity back into OpenAI.[8]

Each step depends on the one before it. Oracle's backlog is only worth something if OpenAI keeps paying. OpenAI can only pay if its revenue grows or investors keep funding it. Nvidia's orders only hold if Oracle and others keep building. This series calls the belief each firm relies on its premise: the thing that must stay true for its own numbers to work. Every layer of the market has one. Each points to someone else.

The last row matters as much as the rest. Software firms and device makers are outside the chain of payments. Their numbers rest on the opposite premise. If AI agents work as promised, seat-based software loses customers. If AI keeps memory scarce, phones and PCs cost more and sell less. The market is betting on both outcomes at once. That is why no firm here is only a winner.

The chain spends about seven times what its end customers pay

One comparison frames the whole series. It sets what users pay the labs against what the industry spends to serve them. The two leading labs now earn about $118B a year between them.[9, 10] OpenAI told investors its annualised revenue was about $50B at the end of September.[9, 11] Anthropic's S-1, its filing to list its shares, shows $16.3B for the first half of 2026.[10] Its amended filing adds $16.96B for the third quarter alone.[12] That is a pace of about $68B a year.

Now compare spending. The five largest spenders plan $803B to $828B of capital spending (capex) for 2026.[13] Not all of it serves the labs. Microsoft, Amazon and Google also rent capacity to ordinary companies. Meta spends for its own ad business. Still, the labs' promises are the largest single source of new demand. OpenAI has committed about $1.4T, which is 28 times its revenue.[14] Anthropic owes about $430B of compute it cannot cancel.[10] That is about six times its third-quarter revenue pace.

A gap this large does not prove the build-out is a mistake. Capacity must be built before anyone can rent it. So in any build-out, spending runs ahead of revenue. The real question is timing. Will revenue arrive before the money that bridges the gap runs out? That bridge money comes from three sources. Each one is a premise of its own.

  • Investors in the labs accept losses today in exchange for value tomorrow. Their patience is already wearing thin. On 30 October OpenAI cut its funding round from $30B to $20B.[15] It also cut its target value from $1.4T to $1.25T.[15]
  • Suppliers fund their own customers. Nvidia put $30B into OpenAI.[8] Google has put about $13B into Anthropic.[16, 10] SoftBank has put $64.6B into OpenAI.[17]
  • Lenders lend against contracts and chips. US data-centre debt is at least $1.3T.[18] Lenders sometimes care more about the credit of a borrower's customers than about the borrower itself.

Three moves turn a belief into money

Read the 92 deal legs side by side and the same three moves keep appearing. Through them, one firm's premise becomes another firm's revenue.

Invest so they can buy. A supplier puts money into a customer. The customer then spends it with the supplier or its partners. Nvidia invested $30B in OpenAI, which buys capacity full of Nvidia chips.[8] Google invested in Anthropic, which committed at least $114.5B to Google Cloud.[16] Amazon put $15B into OpenAI, with $35B more depending on conditions. In return OpenAI committed $138B to AWS.[19, 20] AMD gave OpenAI warrants, rights to buy up to 10% of AMD, as OpenAI deploys AMD GPUs.[21]

Promise to fill the gap. A supplier or partner backstops the customer, promising to pay if the customer cannot. Nvidia agreed to buy up to $6.3B of CoreWeave's unused capacity.[22] It lists $36B of capacity buyback commitments.[8] It also guarantees up to $105B of OpenAI's lease and power payments in Ohio.[23] Google backstops Fluidstack's leases at TeraWulf, Hut 8 and Cipher.[24, 25] Broadcom supports about $31B of debt for a vehicle that buys Google chips for Anthropic.[26, 10]

Turn the contract into collateral. A long contract from a strong customer becomes backlog. The seller then borrows against it. CoreWeave carries about $51.6B of debt, including leases, against $104B of backlog.[27, 28] Hut 8 raised $3.25B of investment-grade project debt against its 15-year lease.[29] In September Lambda closed a $926M term loan backing GPUs for an investment-grade customer.[30]

The first two moves close the loop. Money leaves a supplier and comes back as orders. The third move works differently. It closes nothing. Instead, it lets the loop grow larger than the equity behind it, because each contract becomes the base for new debt. This difference matters later. It explains why the firms that run out of money are the ones that borrowed against contracts, not the ones that sold the chips.

How the risk built up: almost all of it in two years

None of this formed slowly. Of the 92 deal legs since January 2023, only six date from 2023 and 2024. Together they are worth about $20B. The other 86 were signed in 2025 and 2026. Spending followed the same curve. The money paying for it moved from cash to borrowing.

Deals. In 2025 firms signed about $0.9T of binding deals and $0.5T of non-binding ones. In the first ten months of 2026 the figures were $1.5T and $1.4T. The non-binding share rose. More of the chain now rests on promises that can still be withdrawn.

Capex. The four largest spenders averaged about $36B a quarter in 2022.[31] In the first quarter of 2026 they spent about $130B, 3.6 times as much.[31] The FT puts their AI spending at about $1.1T from 2023 to mid-2026. About $745B is planned for 2026 alone, roughly 80% more than in 2025.[32]

Borrowing. CoreWeave's debt rose from $21.4B at the end of 2025 to $35.1B by June.[28, 27] Counting leases, the total reached about $51.6B.[33, 34] US data-centre and related debt passed $1.3T.[18]

The speed is a risk in itself. A chain built over ten years would have survived at least one slowdown. It would have learned where it bends. This chain has never been tested. Almost every contract, loan and valuation in it was set during one long upswing. Each assumes the upswing continues.

How much of the headline money is real

Announced amounts overstate what is actually committed. Nvidia's "up to $100B" for OpenAI was a letter of intent.[8, 35] It became a $30B equity investment.[8, 35] Reports of a $250B guarantee ended as a signed guarantee capped at $105B.[23, 8] Only the first gigawatt of AMD's 6 GW deal with OpenAI is binding.[21] For this reason, every deal leg in the database is marked by how firm it is, from signed contract to stated plan.

In the database, about $1.6T is signed or binding contracts. About $0.4T is closed equity. Another $1.9T is letters of intent, stated plans and talks. Only the binding part feeds the model built in Part 2. It is a fraction of the headlines, but still very large.

Every firm is both culprit and victim

If every firm's numbers rest on another firm's premise, every firm plays two roles at once. This series uses two words for them.

  • Culprit side: how much a firm's own premise supports other firms' numbers. Its spending is their revenue while its investments are their funding. If its premise fails, that support disappears.
  • Victim side: how much a firm's numbers depend on other firms' premises. If its customers, borrowers or the companies it owns prove wrong, it pays.

Three examples show why the two roles cannot be separated.

  • Oracle spent $75.7B on capex in the last twelve months.[33] Its operating cash flow was $46.9B, so it borrowed the difference.[33] That spending is revenue for Nvidia and others: the culprit side. About 46% of its backlog comes from OpenAI: the victim side.[7, 36]
  • Nvidia invested in OpenAI, backstops CoreWeave and guarantees OpenAI's Ohio lease. Those are culprit roles. But its revenue is other firms' capex, which makes it a victim too.
  • SK hynix and Micron are among the biggest winners of the premise. Micron earned $133.2B of revenue in fiscal 2026, with an 86.8% gross margin in its last quarter.[37] Their gain is the cost that device makers pay. And their revenue falls if AI spending slows.

In principle the two roles are clear. Measuring them for every firm is harder. So is finding the premise whose failure would do the most damage. Both need something the headlines never provide: a map of who pays whom across the whole market. Part 2 draws that map from the filings of 59 firms.
